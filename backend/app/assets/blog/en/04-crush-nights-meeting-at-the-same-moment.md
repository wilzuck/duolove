You probably know this scene. You send a message on a Tuesday at ten at night, from your bed, after a long day on the road. The other person doesn't reply. They'll answer on Thursday morning, in a taxi, between two calls, and at that moment you'll be in a meeting. Your Tuesday message has already gone stale. The line that was meant to make them smile lands cold and out of step, like a dish that's been reheated.

Plenty of online connections don't die from a disagreement. They die from bad timing. Both people mean well, both are available, but never at the same moment.

## Time, the currency we spend without counting

People talk a lot about photos, words and profiles. They talk less about time, which is the real currency of meeting someone. Giving someone your attention means giving them a piece of your evening. And a piece of evening given at the very moment you receive one back is worth even more.

Think of a neighbourhood market in Cotonou. What brings it to life isn't the number of stalls, it's that at seven in the morning everyone is there. People haggle, bump into each other, stop at the same chilli seller. If the stalls opened at different times, everyone would wander alone between empty tables.

That's the idea Crushduo puts behind Crush Nights: actually being together at the same moment.

## What Crush Nights are

The principle is simple. The team announces an evening at a fixed time. People who sign up get a reminder so they don't forget. When the time comes, everyone is online together, and that changes everything: searches pay off faster, because the people you come across are there, right now, available, instead of having left the app three days ago.

In practice, it all happens through the Live Crush menu. You run a search based on your criteria. The app suggests people who are online and compatible, you can invite them, and when an invitation is accepted live, the conversation opens. Opening it costs 1 Crush token. To get you started, three tokens are given to you once, as soon as your profile is 100% complete and your email is verified.

Nothing magic, then: a shared room, a time, and people who have all decided to turn up.

## Getting ready: four things to do beforehand

A live evening is a bit like dinner at friends' place. You don't show up empty-handed.

### Choose your criteria calmly

Before the evening, open the search and look at your criteria. Are they too tight? Set ten and you may get nobody. Set none and you'll be swamped. Keep three or four things that really matter, the ones you wouldn't compromise on, and give the rest some room.

Zeinab, in Niamey, had a long list. She cut it down to the essentials: the city, what she's looking for and one value close to her heart. She said, laughing, that she felt lighter, like someone unpacking an overstuffed suitcase.

### Pick a calm evening

Plan for a moment when you're really present. Not on the bus, not between errands, not in front of the TV with the family commenting on the soap. A chair, a glass of water or ginger juice, a decent connection and a charged phone. In Ouagadougou, when a power cut is looming, it's wise to charge up beforehand and have a backup.

### Have your profile ready

Anyone who accepts your invitation will open your profile within seconds. Read it over: photos approved by moderation, a clear bio, answers to your profile questions, values on display. If you've had your profile verified, the verified badge is there to reassure. A half-finished profile, in the middle of the evening, kills the momentum.

### Prepare an opening line

Don't head in with "hey how are you". Prepare two or three conversation starters that sound like you, linked to something you read about the other person: a dish they mention, a trip, a job. Jot them on a scrap of paper or in your notes. You don't need to use them all: knowing they're there calms the nerves.

And if you'd rather have help breaking the ice, the chat offers icebreakers.

## During the evening: the art of patience

It's tempting to do everything at once: send ten invitations, write to five people, read nothing properly. That's a trap. A live evening isn't a speed contest.

Invite sparingly. Wait for the answer. Meanwhile, reread a profile, take a sip, breathe. An invitation left unanswered says nothing about you: the other person may be having dinner, helping a child or hunting for their charger.

When a conversation opens, listen before you tell your own story. Ask a genuinely curious question, then read the answer to the end. Shared time is precious, so don't waste it on a monologue.

> Being in the same moment as someone already tells them something. The rest is how you listen to them.

## Rejection, or how to take it and give it

On an evening when everyone is online, you also get more "no"s. A declined invitation, a conversation that stops, a silence. That's normal, even healthy: it means everyone is free to choose.

**When someone says no to you**, don't chase. Don't ask "why?". Thank them in one line if you like, then move on. Pushing turns a simple no into a bad memory, and nothing is left of the evening but irritation.

**When you're the one saying no**, be direct but not harsh: "Thanks for your message, I don't feel it's a fit, I wish you all the best." You don't owe anyone a justification.

And if someone crosses the line, you can report and block. Using these tools isn't picking a fight, it's basic hygiene. Nobody should stay in a conversation that hurts them.

## When the evening ends: keep a steady pace

It's midnight in Abidjan, or ten at night in Dakar. The evening is over and your head is still spinning a little. What now?

First, don't decide everything that night. A promising conversation can wait until tomorrow. Send a short message to say you enjoyed the chat and suggest carrying on with a fresh head.

Then give yourself a real rhythm. A few conversations at a time are enough, not twenty. Set aside one moment a day to reply, rather than keeping a thread open all the time that nibbles away at your evenings, meals and sleep. When a conversation turns serious, you can propose a date in the conversation and move from the keyboard to a real meeting, in a public place, with someone close to you in the loop.

And if you need a breather, Pause mode is there for that. Stepping back isn't giving up: it's making room to come back keen.

None of them replaces sincerity or patience. They're friendly nudges, not shortcuts.

## What you keep in the morning

A good Crush Night isn't measured by the number of conversations. It's measured by what's left the next day: an exchange that made you smile, someone whose voice you'd like to hear, or simply the pleasure of having been truly present for two hours.

So next time a Crush Night is announced, note the time, prepare two criteria, a lamp, a glass of water and an opening line. The rest happens on the night, at the same moment as someone else.
