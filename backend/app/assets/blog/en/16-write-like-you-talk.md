When you talk face to face, your face does the work: a raised eyebrow, a half-smile, a lingering voice, and the driest sentence becomes tender. In writing, all of that disappears. You're left with words, punctuation and a few emojis to make up for what's missing. Writing "like you talk" isn't an innate talent, then: it's a small art of translation, one you can learn without losing your spontaneity.

Here are a few pointers, drawn from everyday situations, so your messages sound like you.

## Your voice first

Before any technique, one question: how do you really talk? In short sentences, with lots of "honestly" and "hang on"? With images, with your grandmother's proverbs? Your writing gains a lot from sounding like your speech. If you start writing too formally, you'll sound like you're drafting an official letter. If you copy the other person's style, you'll sound like you're playing a part.

Read your message aloud before sending it. If you'd never say it that way to someone in front of you, simplify. "I was delighted to come across your profile" becomes "Your profile made me smile". Nothing is lost, and you gain a lot of warmth.

That doesn't mean abandoning correctness. Careful spelling is a sign of consideration, and a typo isn't a serious offence, but a message full of unreadable abbreviations wears the other person out. Find your balance: natural, not sloppy.

## Length and rhythm

There are two opposite pitfalls. The first: the novel. A long block of text written in one go makes the other person feel they have to fill in a questionnaire. The second: the telegram. "How are you?" "Fine, you?" "Good", repeated three days running, says nothing about anyone.

A good rule: **roughly match the length of the message you received**, then add one thing, just one, to keep it going. A detail from your day, an open question, a small observation. If they write you three lines, don't reply with one word; if they write you a paragraph, you can allow yourself one too.

For longer messages, give them air: one idea per block, a blank line between them. It reads better on a small screen, especially when the network is patchy and text takes a while to load.

Another trap: **sending five messages in a row** to say what would fit in one. Every notification sounds like a demand. If you have several ideas, gather them together, or pick the most interesting one to start with.

### Reply speed

Not replying straight away isn't a sign of disinterest. Life happens: work, the commute, family, a data bundle running out. And replying within seconds, every time, can give an impression of anxious waiting, or of pressure.

A few simple principles:

- **Reply when you have something to say**, not just to "avoid keeping them waiting". A thoughtful message is better than an instant one.
- **If you know you'll be away**, say so: "Busy day, I'll reply tonight." One sentence is enough, and the silence doesn't turn into a question.
- **Don't try to mirror their pace like a strategy game.** Waiting on purpose to "look busy" wears everyone out.
- **If they're taking a while**, avoid chains of follow-ups. One light little message, after a while, is enough: "Thinking of you, all good on your side?"

Incognito mode, depending on your plan, hides your activity status: that can be a relief if you don't like being seen "online" without replying. It's a comfort for you, not a tactic.

## Neither robot nor intruder

There are two ways to put people off from a distance. The first is seeming automatic: generic sentences, interchangeable compliments, standard questions sent in the same way to everyone. "Hi, how are you, what do you do for a living?" reads like a form. To avoid the robot effect, anchor each message in something specific: something on their profile, an answer to one of their profile questions, a detail they mentioned earlier.

The second way is intrusion. Too many messages, too fast, too intimate, personal questions from the first exchanges, insisting when the other person replies briefly. The test is simple: *if I received this message from a stranger, would I be comfortable?* If the answer wavers, slow down.

The icebreakers in the chat can help you start without falling into "hey how's it going": pick one that sounds like you, then carry the conversation on in your own words. An icebreaker isn't a line to recite; it's a door left ajar.

## Punctuation, humour and emojis

### Punctuation, the music of a message

Punctuation carries tone. Here's how it's often read, and how to use it:

- **A full stop** after a short sentence can seem curt: "OK." isn't the same as "OK!" or "OK 🙂". It isn't always intended, but it's felt.
- **Ellipses** can express hesitation, tenderness or irony. Too many leave the other person puzzled: what's going unsaid?
- **Exclamation marks** warm things up, up to a point. One per message is joy; five is jitters.
- **Capital letters** shout. One word in capitals can insist gently; a whole sentence sounds like an argument.
- **Multiple question marks** ("???") sound like a reproach.

None of this is a rulebook. Notice how the other person writes, and adjust gently. If they never use a full stop at the end, their bare "ok" probably isn't cold.

### Humour and emojis

Humour is the best and riskiest tool you have. In writing, irony is the first casualty: without a voice or a face, teasing can come across as criticism. Start with humour that pokes fun at yourself, or at the situation, rather than at the other person. Avoid jibes about looks, origins, family or work, even affectionate ones, until you know each other.

Emojis act as **emotional subtitles**. A little smile after some teasing says "just joking". A laughing face can soften a criticism. But beware of the opposite effect: an emoji can also make a message ambiguous, or seem over the top. A few pointers:

1. One or two per message is a touch. A cluster of emojis is noise.
2. Choose the ones you actually use in life, not the ones you think are expected.
3. Don't count on an emoji to rescue a harsh sentence: if the message hurts, the emoji won't erase it.
4. If in doubt about a symbol, use a word instead.

And if the other person uses an emoji you don't quite understand, ask. "Was that a laugh or a sigh?" is a perfectly charming question.

## Voice notes: a warm voice, in moderation

A voice note is a bridge between writing and a call. It carries what words can't: the smile, the hesitation, the accent, a laugh. When a story would take ages to type, a short voice note tells it better.

But voice notes have their own etiquette. Messages several minutes long, sent without warning, are hard to listen to on a bus or at work. Ask yourself: can they listen to it now? A pleasant habit is to **offer rather than impose**: "I can send you a voice note, or would you rather write?" Keep your voice notes short, speak without reading, and avoid places that are too noisy.

And if the other person prefers writing, don't push. Some people don't like hearing their own voice, or don't have headphones to hand. The day you both want to hear each other, calling will be there, for two people in an active duo.

## Reading the other person's tone

Reading tone in writing is mainly about **resisting the first negative interpretation**. A short message, a late reply, an "ok": you can read coldness into it, but most of the time there's context you don't know about.

A useful habit: come up with three possible explanations before drawing a conclusion. They're busy, they type fast, they're tired. The kindest explanation is often the closest to the truth. And if the doubt persists, ask gently: "I get the feeling I said something clumsy, tell me if that's the case." That sentence is rarely badly received.

Look at **patterns** rather than one-offs, too. Someone who always writes briefly isn't ignoring you. Someone who was warm and suddenly turns curt may deserve being asked if everything's all right.

### Common misunderstandings

A few classic scenes you'll recognise as you read:

- **The cold "haha".** A written laugh can be sincere or polite. Only context can tell, and caution says not to jump to conclusions.
- **The joke that falls flat.** You tease, they answer seriously. Don't over-explain: "I was joking, but I get that it doesn't come across in writing."
- **The question that's too direct.** "What exactly are you looking for?" can sound like an interrogation. Rephrase it with curiosity: "What would you enjoy sharing with someone, right now?"
- **Out-of-sync rhythms.** One writes all day, the other in the evening. Talk about it early: "I'm more available in the evenings, I often reply late, but I haven't forgotten you."
- **The topic that hurts without meaning to.** A remark about family, religion, someone's situation. If you sense things tensing up, slow down and ask.

## Recovering from a blunder

Everyone makes them. What matters is the repair. Three steps almost always make it simple:

1. **Acknowledge it quickly**, without a long justification: "My joke was clumsy, sorry."
2. **State your intention in one sentence**, without pleading: "I meant to tease you, not upset you."
3. **Leave some space.** Don't bombard them with apologies. One message is enough. If they take time to reply, let them.

Avoid "I'm sorry if you took it badly", which pushes the blame back onto them. Prefer "I'm sorry I said that". And if it was a real gaffe, someone comfortable in their own skin will appreciate the sincerity.

If the other person has been clumsy with you, you're also allowed to say so gently: "That message stung a little, I'd rather tell you." Most people are happy to apologise when they're spoken to without accusation. And if an exchange crosses the line, you can report or block.

## Reactions and replying to a specific message

The chat offers two discreet tools that prevent plenty of misunderstandings.

**Reactions** (an emoji placed on a message) are perfect for saying "seen it, liked it" without writing a long reply. A heart, a laugh, a thumbs-up: the message gets a welcome, and the conversation breathes. But don't replace the whole conversation with reactions: when they ask you a real question, a reaction on its own can look like dodging.

**Replying to a specific message** is valuable when several topics overlap. You asked two questions and they only answered one? You can reply by quoting the message in question, and everything becomes clear again. It's also a great way to show you've been listening: picking up on a detail they mentioned three messages earlier proves your attention, and it's a very human gesture.

## To finish

Writing like you talk means accepting being a little imperfect on screen, just as you are in life. Sentences that fit you, a tone the other person can recognise, humour that hurts no one, and the kindness of assuming the best before drawing conclusions: that's more than enough. Before you send your next message, read it aloud. If it sounds like you, send it.
