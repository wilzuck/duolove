The meal went well, the conversation flowed, you laughed. Then the waiter puts the bill on the table, and a small silence falls. Who should pay? Should you offer to split? Insist? Let it happen? For many people, this is one of the most awkward moments of a first date.

There's no universal rule. Habits vary from one family to another, one city to another, between home and the diaspora. This guide helps you see things more clearly, talk about it without unease, and spot the situations where money becomes a warning sign.

## Traditions and their nuances

In many West African families, the custom is that whoever does the inviting, often the man, settles the bill. It's seen as a mark of respect, seriousness and consideration. Many people remain attached to it, and that's entirely legitimate.

Others, particularly younger people or those in the diaspora, prefer to split or take turns. For them, it's a way of showing that you're moving forward as equals.

Between the two, there are countless nuances. Some people insist on paying out of pride, others feel uncomfortable being treated by someone they barely know. Some families expect a specific gesture, others leave everyone free. And when one person grew up in Lomé or Dakar and the other in Paris or Montreal, expectations can differ without anyone being wrong.

None of these approaches is better than another. **What matters is that each person feels respected and at ease.**

## What the bill says (and doesn't say)

People often read a lot into this moment:

- **Paying isn't buying.** Settling the bill creates no debt, emotional or physical. Nobody "owes" anything in return.
- **Offering to split isn't a rejection.** It's neither a lack of interest nor a slight on the other person.
- **Accepting to be treated isn't taking advantage**, as long as it stays reasonable and sincere.

The gesture matters less than **the way it's made**: simply, without calculation, without show.

## The different ways of doing it

### Whoever invites pays

This is the most common option. If you suggest the date, plan to pay, and choose a place that fits your budget. A coffee, a juice on a terrace or a walk followed by a drink are perfect for a first meeting.

### Splitting

Each person pays their share, or you divide it in two. Simple and clear, provided you've talked about it or suggest it naturally.

### Taking turns

One pays the first time, the other the next. An elegant way of balancing things without counting every penny.

### A small gesture in return

If the other person insisted on paying, you can say: "Thank you so much. Next time, it's my treat." It's a thoughtful way of showing you appreciated it, and that you'd like there to be a next time.

## Talking about it simply, before or during

The awkwardness often comes from what goes unsaid. A few phrases are enough to clear it:

- **When suggesting the date**: "Can I take you for a drink on Saturday?" Saying you're taking them out makes it clear who's paying.
- **If you'd rather split**: "Fancy going for a meal together? We'll split it, that way we're free to do it again."
- **When the bill arrives**: "Can I get this?" or "Shall we split it?", said with a smile.
- **If the other person insists on paying**: a sincere thank you is enough. No need to turn it into a debate.

## Choosing a place that puts nobody in a difficult position

A first date isn't a display of wealth. A place that's too luxurious can create awkwardness or false expectations. Go for:

- **A public, lively, well-known place**, where you both feel comfortable.
- **A reasonable budget** for whoever is inviting.
- **A short format**: a coffee or a juice lets you get to know each other without pressure, and consider a next time if there's a spark.

## When money becomes a warning sign

The question of the bill is harmless. Requests for money, on the other hand, never are at the start of a relationship. Be careful if the person:

- **Asks you for money before you've even met**, for transport, phone credit, medical costs or "a little help".
- **Sets up a date then asks for money to get there**, or cancels at the last minute after receiving something.
- **Always picks very expensive places** and expects you to pay for everything, without ever offering anything in return.
- **Makes money a proof of love**: "If you cared about me, you'd help me."
- **Describes an emergency** that requires you to pay immediately.

## Money, later in the relationship

If the relationship grows, the question of money will come back in other forms: outings, gifts, shared plans, family support. The key stays the same: **talk about it early, calmly, without taboo.** Two people who can approach this subject with respect are building a more peaceful future, whatever their habits.

> It isn't who pays that gives a date its value, it's how respected each person feels.

So for your next first date, choose a simple place, say clearly whether you're inviting or would rather split, and welcome the other person's answer with a smile. The rest of the evening will be far more enjoyable once the bill is settled, and you can focus on what really matters: getting to know each other.
