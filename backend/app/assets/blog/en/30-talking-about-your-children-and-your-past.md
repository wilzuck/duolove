On many profiles, one question hangs silently: "When should I say I have children? That I've been married? That I've been through something heavy?" Fear of rejection pushes people to delay, sometimes to keep quiet. But the longer you wait, the harder the admission becomes, and the more the other person feels deceived.

The right approach is neither to say everything at once nor to hide everything. It's to **say the essentials early, simply, and keep the details for when trust is there**.

## What should be said early

Some information shapes the future of a relationship. Keeping it back deprives the other person of an informed choice:

- **Your children**, if you have any: their existence changes your availability, your priorities, your future.
- **Your real marital status**: single, divorced, widowed, separated. A separation in progress is not a divorce.
- **A commitment or a parallel relationship**, whatever form it takes.
- **An incompatible life plan**: wanting or not wanting more children, a plan to move abroad, heavy family obligations.

These points are **facts**, not intimate confidences. Saying them doesn't require telling your story: one sentence is enough.

## What you can keep for later

On the other hand, you don't have to tell everything:

- the detailed **reasons** for a divorce or a break-up;
- **personal ordeals** (illness, bereavement, violence you've suffered) until you're ready to talk about them;
- **financial** or family details that don't concern the other person yet;
- anything that could be used to **locate you** or identify your children (schools, timetables, addresses).

A useful rule: you share **what affects the other person**, not **what still hurts you**. The second will come naturally when trust is there.

## How to say it: three model sentences

How you say it matters as much as what you say. Stay simple, factual and calm, without apologising:

- **Children**: "I'm a mum of two, aged 6 and 9. They're very important to me, and I'd rather tell you straight away."
- **Divorce**: "I was married for a few years; we've been divorced for three years now. It's a closed chapter, and I'm ready to write the next one."
- **Being widowed**: "I lost my husband two years ago. I took the time I needed, and I feel ready to meet someone."

These sentences have three qualities: they're **clear**, they show you've **made peace** with that part of your story, and they **hand the conversation back** to the other person.

## When?

There's no exact moment, but there are a few guidelines:

- **For children and marital status**: ideally on your profile or in your first exchanges. It avoids dates doomed to fail and attracts people who already accept this reality.
- **For more personal ordeals**: once trust is established, often after a few weeks of regular conversation or meetings. You choose the moment, not the other person.
- **For sensitive subjects** (violence, health, debt): when you feel safe, and never under pressure.

If the other person asks you something directly, answer honestly, even briefly. Saying "I'd rather tell you about it in person, later" is completely legitimate and won't come across as hiding something, as long as the essentials haven't been concealed.

## If the other person reacts badly

Sometimes the reaction isn't the one you hoped for: silence, a clumsy remark, a withdrawal. A few reminders:

- **It isn't a judgement on your worth.** It's information about what the other person is ready to accept.
- **An early exit beats a long misunderstanding.** You spare everyone months of effort.
- **Don't try to convince them.** A relationship that requires you to justify a fact of your life is off to a bad start.

Conversely, a kind reaction ("thank you for telling me", "I'd like to know more whenever you're ready") is a very good sign of maturity.

## When the other person opens up

You'll also, in turn, be the one receiving a confidence. A few habits to welcome it well:

- **Thank them for their trust.** A simple "thank you for telling me" already goes a long way.
- **Ask open questions, without unhealthy curiosity.** "Do you want to talk about it?" rather than "Why did it end badly?"
- **Don't compare** with your own story to play it down or dramatise it.
- **Respect their pace.** If they stop, don't push.
- **Keep the confidence.** It isn't to be shared with your friends without their agreement.

## Children: the pace of introductions

If you're a parent, you'll also have to decide when to introduce your partner to your children. Here again, it's best to **take your time**: children get attached quickly, and a meeting that comes too soon, followed by a break-up, hurts them. Many parents wait several months of a stable relationship before arranging a first meeting, short, in a simple setting and without any staging.

## Questions you're likely to be asked

When you mention a past relationship or children, some questions come up almost every time. Anticipating them saves you from being caught off guard:

- **"Are you still on good terms?"** Answer simply, without running down the other parent or casting yourself as the victim: "We get along for the children's sake" or "We've gone our separate ways" is enough.
- **"Why did it end?"** You can answer in one sentence and keep the rest: "We weren't heading in the same direction any more." If someone insists from the very first exchanges, that's curiosity to keep in check: nothing obliges you to submit to an interrogation.
- **"Do you want more children?"** This is a genuine life-plan question, and it's best answered frankly, even if the answer is "I don't know yet".
- **"Do your children live with you?"** A factual answer is enough, with no details of arrangements or timetables.

Answering calmly and briefly shows that you're at peace with your story, which is far more reassuring than a long account.

## Finding the balance

What makes this subject delicate is that it touches our vulnerability. Yet it's often by telling the truth, calmly, that you filter best: the people who stay are the ones who accept your whole story, not a tidied-up version.

> Telling the truth early isn't stripping yourself bare: it's choosing people with whom you can stay clothed in sincerity.

And remember that your past is part of what made you. You don't have to apologise for it: only to share it when it's needed, in the way it's needed.
