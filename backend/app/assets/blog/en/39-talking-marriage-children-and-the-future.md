You're on Crushduo for a serious relationship. You know that one day you want to get married, maybe have children, build a stable life. And yet, when the moment comes to talk about it, a little voice holds you back: "If I bring up marriage now, I'll scare them away."

That hesitation is understandable. But never talking about the future carries its own risk: finding out months later that you don't want the same things at all. The good news is that there's a way to approach these subjects **without pressure, at the right pace and with respect**. Here's how.

## Why talking about it isn't "too soon"

When you're looking for a serious relationship, life plans aren't a detail. They're part of compatibility, just like values or character.

- **You save time**: two people who want opposite things find out before they get too attached.
- **You show you're serious**: talking calmly about the future often reassures more than it frightens.
- **You avoid the unspoken things** that turn into resentment.

The key isn't to avoid the subject but to **pace it**. You don't ask for a wedding date over a first coffee. You can, however, find out fairly quickly whether the other person sees marriage in their future at all.

## Start with your profile

Your profile is the first place to set the tone, so you don't have to repeat it in every conversation. In your bio, your **values** and your answers to the **profile questions**, you can say simply what you're looking for:

- "I'm looking for a serious relationship and would like to build a family."
- "Marriage is part of my plans, with no rush."
- "I already have children and I'm open to having more."

Lines like these attract people who are on the same page and gently discourage those looking for something else. It's a kindness to everyone.

## The right moment, step by step

There's no universal timetable, but a natural progression helps a lot.

### In the first messages

Stick to the broad strokes: what each of you is looking for, a serious relationship or not, a wish to marry or not. A light question is enough: "Do you see yourself married in a few years?"

### After a few dates

When the interest is mutual, you can go further: children, where to live, the role of family, career. The tone stays that of a conversation, not a questionnaire.

### When the relationship becomes exclusive

This is the time for the concrete subjects: timing, meeting each other's families, traditions, how you'll organise money. You're building a plan together.

## Children: wishes, how many, when

The question of children deserves to be cleared up fairly early, because it's rarely negotiable.

- **Do you want them?** Yes, no, maybe, already a parent?
- **When?** Soon after marriage, in a few years, with no fixed timeline?
- **How do you picture raising them?** Values, religion, languages spoken at home, school.
- **If one of you already has children**, how do you imagine a blended family?

Approach it gently: some people are living through delicate personal situations on this subject. An open question, "How do you picture your family later on?", leaves the other person free to answer at their own pace.

## Where to live: city, country, diaspora

For couples split between two cities, two countries, or between Africa and the diaspora, the question of where to live is central.

- **Where does each of you see yourself in five years?** Staying in your city, moving back home, going abroad?
- **Who is ready to move**, and on what terms (work, studies, family)?
- **What role for ageing parents**: living nearby, taking them in, supporting them from a distance?

These questions don't need a final answer straight away, but they show whether your paths can come together.

## Bride price and family traditions

In many of our families, marriage isn't just about two people but two families. The bride price (the dot, in many francophone countries), introduction visits, and customary, religious and civil ceremonies each have their importance, and they vary a great deal from one region or community to another.

- **Find out about the other family's customs** rather than assuming they're like yours.
- **Talk about what matters to each of you**: a big ceremony, a simple celebration, strict respect for tradition, a compromise.
- **Raise the subject without embarrassment**, once the plan becomes real, ideally before the families get involved.
- **Keep one rule of caution**: bride price and traditions are discussed between families who know each other, never by message with someone you haven't yet met. A request for money "for the bride price" or "to prepare the wedding" from someone you've never seen is a warning sign, not proof of commitment.

## Questions to ask each other

Once the relationship is settling in, take a calm moment to take stock together. A few ideas:

- **Do we share a common vision of marriage** and of each person's place in the couple?
- **Do we agree about children**, how many and how to raise them?
- **Where do we want to live**, and how do we get there?
- **How will we manage money** and our obligations to our families?
- **What do we expect from our families**, and what do we expect of ourselves in dealing with them?

You don't have to agree on everything. What matters is **knowing your differences** and whether you can make room for them.

## What scares people off, and what reassures

Some attitudes turn a useful conversation into a source of anxiety:

- **What scares people off**: giving an ultimatum, demanding an immediate answer, comparing yourselves with other couples, putting family pressure front and centre.
- **What reassures**: talking about yourself before questioning the other person, asking open questions, accepting an "I don't know yet", coming back to the subject later without resentment.

And if the other person always dodges, changes the subject every time, or promises everything without ever following through, that's an answer too.

> Talking about the future doesn't scare off the right people: it helps them stay.

You have the right to know what you're building. With tact, patience and sincere questions, these conversations become moments of closeness rather than ordeals. Start small, listen a lot, and let your shared plans take shape step by step.
