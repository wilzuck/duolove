When you open Crushduo for the first time, the "About me" box feels like an exam. Five hundred characters to sum yourself up is both a lot and very little. So you write "I'm a simple person, I love laughing, travelling and good food", you save it, and you wonder why nobody has a question to ask.

The problem isn't the length. It's that this sentence could describe almost anyone. A good bio doesn't try to sum you up: it tries to give the other person **something to hold on to**, a detail they can respond to. Let's see how to build one without forcing it.

## What your bio should do (and shouldn't)

It has three modest jobs:

- **Say who you are today**, not who you'd like to be: your life, your tastes, your rhythm.
- **Say what you're looking for**, in one clear sentence, without conditions in capital letters.
- **Open a door**: a curious detail, a question, a habit that makes people want to react.

It shouldn't: tell your romantic history, explain why your previous relationships failed, list what you can't stand, or serve as a set of specifications. Those subjects will come up in conversation, once trust is there.

## The three-step method

### 1. A specific fact, not a quality

Qualities ("serious", "nice", "open-minded") prove nothing; everyone claims them. Facts, on the other hand, sketch a real person.

- Instead of "I love cooking": "On Sundays I make attiéké with fish for the whole compound, and nobody lets me forget the chilli."
- Instead of "I'm sporty": "A run along the seafront at six, before the city wakes up."
- Instead of "I love travelling": "Next destination: Grand-Popo, with or without company."

A specific fact doesn't need to be spectacular. It needs to be true, and a little bit yours.

### 2. A clear intention, said gently

Say what you're looking for in simple words: "I'm looking for a serious relationship, with someone who loves building plans together", or "I'd like to get to know someone first, with no pressure." This clarity saves everyone time. It also avoids misunderstandings: the person reading knows straight away whether you're on the same wavelength.

Avoid negative phrasing ("No games, no liars, no time-wasters"). It comes across as a hurt person on the defensive, not someone who is keen to meet people.

### 3. An open door

Finish with a sentence that invites a reply. A light question works very well:

- "And you, what was the best dish of your childhood?"
- "Where was the last place you laughed until you cried?"
- "If you could pick just one song for a night journey, what would it be?"

It's the best cure for an empty first message: you hand your future match a topic in advance.

## A before and after example

**Before**: "I'm a simple, serious woman who loves to laugh. Looking for a serious man, no games please. I like music and films."

**After**: "Nurse in Cotonou, on call every other weekend. I unwind with gospel music in the kitchen and Nollywood films that I comment on out loud (yes, it's unbearable). Looking for a serious relationship with someone who knows how to listen. And you, what's your cult film?"

The second version is about 280 characters. It stays modest, exaggerates nothing, and offers at least four ideas for a first question: the job, gospel, Nollywood, the cult film.

## The most common mistakes

- **Copying a text you found online.** Stock phrases are easy to spot, and the person replying feels as if they're talking to a template.
- **Writing a novel.** On a phone, a dense paragraph is off-putting. Leave some air: two or three short sentences beat a block of text.
- **Small lies.** A rounded-down age, an embellished job, a few extra centimetres: all of this comes out on the first date and ruins trust. Write what you can stand behind face to face.
- **Talking about the other person more than yourself.** "Looking for a tall, rich, religious, funny man" describes an identikit picture, not a person. Describe what you offer as much as what you expect.
- **Leaving typos everywhere.** It isn't about writing perfectly, but a quick read-through out loud fixes almost everything.

## Write in the language that sounds like you

The English, or French, you speak every day is the right one. A local expression, a word from your city or a touch of humour can add character, as long as they stay understandable to someone from another country. If you speak Fon, Wolof, Hausa or another language, you can mention it in your profile: it creates a warm point of connection with people who share it.

## How much time should you spend on it?

Twenty minutes, just once, is enough. Write first without censoring yourself, then cut. Reread it the next day: what makes you smile stays, what sounds hollow goes. You can always edit it later in your profile, and it's even a good idea to update it as your life changes.

## A quick test before you save

Ask yourself three questions:

1. Can someone who doesn't know me guess **one specific thing** about me?
2. Have I said **clearly** what I'm looking for?
3. Have I left **an open door** for conversation?

If the answer is yes three times, your bio is ready. It doesn't need to be brilliant: it needs to be true, lively and welcoming. The rest happens in conversation, where your personality counts far more than any text.

> A good bio doesn't make people say "what a beautiful text", but "I want to reply to them".

And if you're still unsure, say it out loud, as if you were talking to a friend. What you'd say naturally is almost always better than what you'd write while trying hard.
