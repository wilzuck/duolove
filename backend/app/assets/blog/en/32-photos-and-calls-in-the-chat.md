A conversation that's going well almost always spills beyond text: you want to see the other person's face, hear their voice, share a photo from your day. These gestures bring you closer, but they also commit you: a photo you send is out of your control, a call reveals the room behind you, and a pushy request can leave you uncomfortable.

Here are some pointers to move at your own pace, set your boundaries without conflict, and stay in control of your image.

## Photos: what's worth sharing

Depending on your plan, you can send photos in the conversation. They're useful for showing a place, a dish, a moment from your life: little pieces of everyday life that give substance to a new connection.

A few good habits:

- **Share what you'd be comfortable seeing passed around.** Anything you send can be saved, forwarded or screenshotted. Ask yourself: "If this picture ended up somewhere else, would I be OK with it?"
- **Avoid identifying details**: number plates, a visible address, your children's school uniform, the letterhead on a document.
- **Never give intimate images to someone you haven't met in person.** Even if trust seems to be there. Images like these can be used to pressure or humiliate.
- **Keep your own tempo.** Nobody has to send a photo, or respond to a request straight away.

## When someone asks for a photo that makes you uneasy

Some requests go beyond what you want: a more revealing photo, a photo "proving" you're really the person in the profile, a photo of your ID. You have the right to say no, simply and without justifying yourself:

- "I don't share that kind of photo, but I'm happy to show you a picture of my day."
- "I'd rather we did a video call, it's an easier way to know who's who."

If the person insists, plays on guilt ("you don't trust me") or threatens you ("I'll show people our messages"), it's no longer about trust: it's **pressure**. In that case:

1. **Don't give in**, and don't send anything else.
2. **Keep the messages** as evidence.
3. **Report and block**. Moderation takes this kind of behaviour very seriously.

## Voice and video calls: an important milestone

A call is a valuable step. The voice, the laugh, the way someone reacts in real time tell you things no message can. It also helps confirm that the person really is the one in the photos, which is excellent protection against fake profiles.

### Preparing the call

- **Suggest it and agree on a time.** An unexpected call can startle or embarrass.
- **Pick a quiet spot** with a decent connection. Bad reception can spoil an otherwise lovely chat.
- **Think about your background**: nothing personal or identifying behind you (post, family photos, an address).
- **Start with audio** if video worries you: you can switch to video next time.
- **Keep it short** the first time: twenty minutes is plenty. Ending on a high is better than running out of steam.

### During the call

- Be **natural**: pauses, hesitations and awkward laughs are completely normal.
- Ask **open questions**, and leave room for the answers.
- **Don't record** the call without the other person's agreement, and don't accept being recorded unless you've chosen to.
- **Make mental notes**: what you liked, what surprised you, what bothered you.

## Warning signs around calls

Some behaviour should make you slow down:

- **Repeatedly refusing any call**, with a different excuse each time: one of the most common signs of a fake profile.
- **A call that turns into a request for money** or for something intimate: hang up.
- **Someone who records or takes screenshots** without telling you, then hints at it.
- **Pressure to move to another app** before you've talked properly here: take your time.

Discomfort is information: you don't need to justify it to end a call.

## Setting boundaries without killing the momentum

Saying "no" or "not yet" is a skill, not an attack. A few phrases that work well:

- "I really enjoy our chats, and I need to take things a bit more slowly."
- "I'd rather save that for when we meet."
- "That's not what I'm looking for right now, thanks for understanding."

A kind person will take these words in their stride. Someone who reacts badly gives you valuable information about how they respect boundaries.

## And when you're the one asking?

Be just as attentive to the other person:

- **Ask before sending** anything personal.
- **Accept a no without comment or pushing.** "Not now" is a complete answer.
- **Never try to get a picture or a call by applying pressure**, even through humour or teasing.

## A real-life case: the call that doesn't go to plan

Sometimes a call is disappointing: the voice isn't what you imagined, the conversation drags, the spark isn't there. A few simple reflexes:

- **Don't force it.** You don't have to last twenty minutes if ten were enough to form an impression.
- **End it politely**: "It was nice to hear your voice. I'm going to head off now."
- **Take a moment for yourself** before replying to any message from them. Your first gut reaction isn't always right, in either direction.
- **Talk to someone** if the call left you uneasy: putting words to the feeling helps you see whether it came from the situation or the person.

A call that doesn't meet your expectations isn't a failure: it has saved you weeks of hoping for nothing.

## What consent means

Consent isn't given once and for all. You can agree to a photo one day and refuse the next, enjoy a call and not want video, move quickly with one person and slowly with another. It's reassessed at every step, and everyone has the right to change their mind.

> A healthy connection is one where each person can say no without fear of losing the other.

Treat these pointers as guardrails, not as rules to follow out of fear. Most conversations go very well, and it's precisely by knowing your limits that you can throw yourself into them with confidence.
