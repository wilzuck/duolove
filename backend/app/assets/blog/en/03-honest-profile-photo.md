There's a moment nobody talks about: the moment someone opens your profile for the first time. They haven't read your bio yet. They know nothing about your values or your answers to the personality questions. They look at your photos, often in less time than it takes to hand back change at the market. And yet, in those few seconds, they're already forming an impression: this person seems to laugh easily, take their time, go out, stay in, have friends, love their neighbourhood.

That impression may be wrong. But it will be there. So you might as well take a little care over it, without putting on a disguise.

## What a gallery says without saying anything

A gallery isn't a shop window, it's a small, untidy story. Each image answers, without you knowing, questions the other person doesn't dare to ask: does this person look like what they write? Are they comfortable in their body, their city, their life?

Take Abla, in Lomé. Her first photo is a portrait taken by the living-room window in the morning, when the light comes in at an angle. Nothing extraordinary. But you can see she's looking at the lens the way you look at someone you know. The second photo shows her at a christening, in a wax-print outfit she chose with care, bursting out laughing half in profile. The third is her favourite bench by the beach road. Three images, and already there's a person.

It isn't beauty that does this. It's the consistency between what the image shows and what it lets you guess.

## Light, framing and smile, no complicated technique

You don't need a good camera. A few simple habits do most of the work.

**Light.** Look for morning or late-afternoon light, the kind that softens faces. Full midday sun in Niamey or Ouagadougou flattens everything: eyes squint, shadows fall under the nose. Stand in the shade of a light-coloured wall, facing the light rather than with your back to it. Backlighting, when the sun is behind you, turns a face into a silhouette.

**Framing.** A photo taken from too far away hides your face; one taken too close at arm's length distorts it. Ask a friend to photograph you from about one and a half metres, phone at eye level. Leave some space around your head.

**Smile.** A real smile isn't the one you put on for the camera, it's the one that comes when someone says something silly just before the click. Ask your friend to tell you something funny while taking ten shots. Keep the seventh. A calm, closed-mouth smile is fine too: a relaxed face is better than a forced grin.

## The background, a quiet narrator

There's always something behind you, and it speaks. A messy bedroom wall says something different from a family courtyard on a Sunday, a market stall, an Abidjan pavement or the terrace of a maquis, a local open-air eatery.

Keep it simple. A calm background brings out your face; a busy one pulls attention away. For a portrait, avoid the bathroom mirror and the car's rear-view mirror in particular: mostly they show the effort to look good. Choose a setting where you genuinely feel good: a courtyard, an armchair, a staircase, a street corner you love.

## Group photos and travel photos: friends or traps

Group photos are tricky. They're reassuring, because they show you have people around you. But if it's the first image in your gallery, the other person spends their time wondering which one is you. Put it third or fourth instead, and choose one where you're easy to spot: you're in the middle, or your outfit makes you stand out. Also avoid making someone from your circle recognisable without their consent; ask them, it's the least you can do.

Travel photos have a surprising power: they give you something to talk about. A shot taken on the Dakar ferry, on a country road in Benin, or in a Brussels station on a rainy day offers the other person a way in: "Where was that? Was it good?" But watch out for the catalogue effect. Ten photos in front of ten monuments, and all anyone sees is the monuments. One is enough, ideally one where you can tell you were really there, tired, happy, bag on your shoulder.

## Why the "too perfect" photo backfires

Heavily retouched images, filters that smooth the skin, magazine poses: the urge is understandable. We're afraid of not being liked. But there's a price, and it's paid late, at the moment you meet.

When the person arrives at the date and doesn't find the face from the screen, they don't think "this is a normal person". They think "I was misled". The disappointment doesn't come from your face, which may be lovely, but from the gap. And that gap damages trust before the conversation has even started.

A slightly imperfect photo does the opposite. A strand of hair out of place, a hand in motion, eyes laughing just off camera: all of that says "here's someone alive". In the end, that's what we're looking for behind a screen.

> An honest photo isn't trying to win a contest, it's trying to be recognised at the restaurant door.

## How many photos, and which ones

Four to six photos is a good balance. Fewer, and people are left wanting more. More, and the message gets diluted, and you often keep weak images that drag down the good ones.

Here are three possible sets, for three made-up people.

**Koffi's set, Abidjan, rather reserved.**

1. A portrait in soft light, in front of a light wall, calm smile.
2. A full-length photo, sitting on a low courtyard wall, relaxed.
3. A photo of him cooking, focused, wooden spoon in hand.
4. A photo with two friends, him on the left, easy to recognise.
5. A street photo taken one weekend, unposed.

**Fatou's set, Dakar, very sociable.**

1. A sharp portrait in late-afternoon light.
2. A full-length photo in an outfit she loves, away from any studio backdrop.
3. A family celebration, a burst of laughter caught in the moment.
4. A travel memory where you can feel the happy tiredness.
5. An image of her passion, drawing, with a sketchbook on her knees.
6. A group photo, with her in the middle.

**Idriss's set, in the diaspora in Montreal.**

1. An outdoor portrait, woolly hat on because it's cold, and a big smile.
2. A photo of him at the market on Saturday, arms full of vegetables.
3. A shot from a trip back home, on the road to Cotonou, showing where he comes from.
4. A full-length photo, standing straight, no filter.

Notice what comes up each time: a clear portrait first, a full-length image, a slice of life, and never two near-identical photos.

## Preparing to meet: being the same in person

The point of a gallery isn't to charm at all costs, it's to prepare a date where the other person thinks "oh yes, that's definitely you". A few habits help.

- **Recent photos.** An image from four years ago, with a different haircut, a different weight, a different life, creates a mismatch. Update at least when your look changes.
- **A picture of you today.** If you've cut your hair or let it grow, add a photo that shows it.
- **No surprises on the essentials.** Glasses, a beard, religious or traditional clothing you wear every day: show them.
- **A line for the big day.** In your first message, if it helps, just say: "I'm a bit taller than in my photos." A touch of humour defuses a lot of worries.

For the date itself, proposing a date in the conversation can help you set up the meeting. Choose a public place, tell a friend, and let the conversation do the rest.

## One last idea to take with you

Before publishing, put your phone down and ask someone close to you to look through your gallery without saying anything. Then ask them: "If you'd only seen me in these photos, would you have recognised me?" If they hesitate, replace the image that makes them hesitate. If they smile, your gallery looks like you.
