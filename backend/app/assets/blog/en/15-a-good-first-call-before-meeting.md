Some conversations flow beautifully in writing and fall apart in the first second of voice. Others, shyer on screen, light up as soon as you hear the other person laugh. Nobody knows in advance. That is exactly why a first call deserves a little preparation, without turning it into an ordeal.

On Crushduo, voice and video calls are available between two people who form an active duo, and only them. So you never have to worry about a stranger ringing you out of the blue: if a call is possible, it means you have each already said yes to this connection. That is a good starting point, because it removes half the fear.

## Why a call reassures (both ways)

A message can be reread, corrected, polished with a friend's help. A voice cannot. It carries the tiredness of the day, the hesitation, the smile you hear before you see it. When a call goes well, you come away with an impression that is hard to create in writing: *this person really exists, and this is what they are like.*

A call also reassures people who doubt themselves. Many people write wonderfully and dread speaking, or the reverse. By giving yourself a short, simple frame, you learn not to confuse ease with worth. Someone who stumbles over the first thirty seconds is no less sincere than someone who strings perfect sentences together.

Finally, there is the simple relief of a gentle check. Photos approved by moderation and the verified badge already give you some landmarks, but a live conversation adds what neither can provide: consistency between what is written and what is said, with nothing recorded. Calls go through a third-party service and nothing is kept, so you can speak freely.

## When to suggest it

Not too early, not too late. A simple sign: you catch yourself writing long messages, waiting for replies, wanting to explain something that would be easier to say. That is often the moment.

A few situations where the suggestion comes naturally:

- you have discovered something in common that deserves to be told out loud (a song, a dish, a school memory);
- a joke has almost made it into speech already, and you both write "I wish you could have heard that";
- a date is taking shape and you would like to arrive without nerves;
- you are apart, one in one city and the other in another country, and the time difference makes the messages a bit disjointed.

The wording matters more than the timing. Avoid a "shall we call?" thrown out like a test. Go for an invitation with a way out: "If you fancy it, we could do a quick ten-minute call tonight or tomorrow, just to put a voice to the name. And if you'd rather wait, no problem at all." Mentioning the length is reassuring, so is the "no problem at all", and the person can genuinely say no without feeling guilty.

> A call offered with a way out is never refused: it is chosen.

If the other person says "not now", hear it as information about their pace, not a judgement on you. You can simply reply: "No problem, let's carry on here." The question may come back on its own.

## Setting the scene, without overdoing it

You don't need a studio. But three details change everything.

### Light

On video, face the light source, not the window behind you: otherwise you become a silhouette. In the evening, a lamp placed behind the phone is enough. Look for a simple background, a wall, a curtain, a corner of the living room tidied in thirty seconds. What people see of your home does not need to be beautiful, just calm.

### Noise

Noise gives you away more than the picture does. A television in the next room, a generator, traffic, a family conversation: you don't notice it at home, but the other person hears it all. Choose a quiet corner, or warn them kindly: "I'm somewhere a bit lively, I'll call you back in a quarter of an hour if you'd like us to have some quiet." Nobody will hold it against you.

If audio is enough for you, say so. Many people prefer to start with voice alone: less to look at, less stage fright. You can switch to video later, if you both feel like it.

### Network

Mobile connections can be temperamental, and there is no shame in saying so. A few habits that help:

1. Check your signal before calling, and move near a window if needed.
2. Close data-hungry apps running in the background.
3. If the picture keeps freezing, suggest switching to audio: a clear voice beats a frozen face.
4. Keep your battery charged, or your charger within reach.

Also tell the other person what might happen: "My network is a bit unpredictable sometimes, if it cuts out I'll call you straight back." That simple sentence defuses the awkwardness of a dropped call in advance.

## The first seconds, and how long to talk

The first seconds are the clumsiest, and that is normal. You wonder whether the other person can hear you properly, you laugh nervously, you say "hello" twice. Let that awkwardness exist. You can even name it: "I'm a bit shy, I'd rather tell you." It instantly lightens the mood.

Keep the call short. A first call of about a quarter of an hour, or even less, leaves a pleasant taste of unfinished business, and that is exactly what you want: the urge to do it again. Calls that drag on often run out of steam, and one of you ends up glancing at the time without daring to say so.

Say it at the start: "I'm free for twenty minutes or so." That sets a frame without coldness, and you can both relax knowing it won't spiral.

## When silence settles in

Silence is the number one fear of a first call. Yet in writing, you cope very well with pauses between messages. On the phone, two seconds of nothing feel like an eternity because you can hear them.

Good news: a silence is not a failure. It is often a sign that one of you is thinking about what to say. A few resources to keep to hand, without reciting them:

- **Go back to a detail from the chat**: "You mentioned your Saturday market, what's the thing you always buy there?"
- **Ask a question that calls for a scene**, not a yes or no: "What was the best part of your day?"
- **Use the Question of the day** or one of the profile questions you saw on their profile as an excuse: "Your answer to that question made me smile, tell me more."
- **Simply name the silence**: "Haha, the pause... I was thinking about what you just said." You laugh, you start again.

And if it really doesn't get going again, it doesn't matter. Sometimes two people who write very well are simply less at ease out loud, or a tired day spoils the exchange. A second try, another time, another day are perfectly allowed.

## What to notice without judging

A call gives you a lot of information, but not all the information you might think. Here is what deserves a little attention, and what is better left aside.

**How they listen.** Do they let you finish your sentences? Do they pick up on what you just said, or wait for their turn to go back to their own thread? Someone who repeats back a detail of your story is telling you, without knowing it, that they are listening.

**Their laugh.** Not its volume, its fit: are they laughing with you or at you? Is it a laugh that comes when things are going well, or a nervous laugh hiding something else? Both are human, but a laugh aimed towards you rather than against you is quickly recognised.

**Respect for the frame.** Did they turn up on time, or let you know? Do they respect what you said about how long you had? These small details say more than a big speech.

**How you feel afterwards.** Lighter, more curious? Or strangely tired, without knowing why? That feeling counts as much as the content.

On the other hand, it is better not to judge too quickly on an accent, a voice higher or deeper than expected, the background or ordinary shyness. A voice that doesn't sound like the one you imagined is not a flaw: it is a gap between the story you told yourself and reality. Give your imagination ten minutes to adjust.

And if something makes you uncomfortable, pressure, an overly intimate question, a push to move to another platform, you have the right to hang up. You can also report or block the person from the app. A feeling of unease, even one you can't fully explain, is reason enough.

## If the connection drops

It will happen sooner or later. A call that cuts out mid-sentence is often funnier than dramatic, as long as you don't turn it into a drama.

- **Don't try to work out whose fault it was.** Nobody hung up on purpose. Wait a few moments before calling back so your calls don't cross.
- **Decide in advance who calls back.** A simple convention: whoever started the call calls back. Without that rule, you end up calling each other at the same time, and laughing even more.
- **If it won't connect any more, write.** A short message is enough: "My network is playing up, shall we try again tomorrow at the same time?" Clear, light, no reproach.
- **Learn the lesson without dramatising.** If it keeps happening, switch to audio or change location.

A dropped call, handled well, almost becomes a shared memory. "Remember the time your phone left us hanging right in the middle of a joke?" There are worse first shared stories.

## Ending the call and saying what you thought

Many calls end badly, not because they were bad, but because people don't know how to wrap them up. You drag it out, repeat "right then...", apologise. Instead, prepare a clean exit, flagged a little beforehand:

> "We're getting close to the time I'd set myself, I'll tell you one more thing and then let you go."

Then finish with one sincere, short sentence that promises nothing excessive: "I had a lovely time, thank you for giving it a go." You don't have to fix the next step straight away. But if you would like to do it again, say so plainly: "I'd happily call again."

After the call, let a little time pass, then send a message. It doesn't need to be long. Honest feedback, for example: "That felt more natural than I feared, thank you for that." Or, if there was no spark: "I enjoyed talking, but I don't think this is what I'm looking for. I wish you some lovely encounters." A clear, kind answer is better than a silence that stretches on. And if the other person sends you that kind of message, welcome it as a gift of honesty, not a rejection.

## In practice

Remember three things: a short call, a simple setting, a way out for each of you. The rest belongs to the meeting itself. If you wait to be perfect before calling, you will never call; better a slightly clumsy first try, in a quiet corner with good light, than an endless conversation without a voice. And if you are still hesitating, just write: "What if we put a voice to all this?" The worst that can happen is a good fit of the giggles.
