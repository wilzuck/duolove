Sunday afternoon, and the heat is pressing down on the courtyard. You scroll through profiles between sips of bissap, the chilled hibiscus drink, and one of them stops you. A bright photo, three personality answers, a list of interests. In ten seconds you have already built a whole person: their character, their family, the way they laugh. And that is where the problem starts, because a profile doesn't contain a person. It contains clues.

Knowing how to read them saves time, spares you needless disappointment, and helps you write messages that land. Here is a way to look at a profile with curiosity, without naivety and without harshness.

## A profile is a conversation already begun, not a file

Let's start by shifting how we look. A profile isn't a CV to be assessed. It's someone who has said a few sentences out loud, hoping someone will answer. Some people write with ease; others have just ticked the essentials between two errands. Neither way, on its own, tells you who the person is.

This idea changes how you read: instead of looking for what's wrong, you look for what makes you want to reply. A profile read well almost becomes the start of a dialogue.

## Personality answers: read the tone as much as the content

When someone answers a personality question, three things are visible: what they choose to say, how they say it, and what they skip.

Take a short answer: "My ideal Sunday: sleeping in, then cooking for everyone." It tells you about a rhythm (rest), a taste (cooking) and an attitude (sharing). It doesn't say the person is generous in every circumstance. It simply says what they'd like you to know.

To get something useful out of it:

- **Note the concrete detail.** "I love travelling" says almost nothing. "I took the night bus just to see the river at sunrise" tells you about a person.
- **Listen to the colour.** An answer full of humour, tenderness, seriousness or reserve tells you which register the conversation will be comfortable in.
- **Tell the mood of the day apart from the person.** People answer a question on a tired evening or a cheerful morning. An answer isn't an identity.

What answers don't tell you: how the person behaves when they're upset, how they treat the people they meet, whether they keep their word. Only conversation will show that, and time.

## Values: a direction, not a promise

The values shown on a profile give you a heading. Someone who puts family, faith, ambition or freedom first is telling you where they put their energy. That's precious, because these are often the real deep subjects of a relationship.

But watch out for two traps. The first: reading a value as a complete programme. "Family" can mean five very different things depending on who writes it: being close to your parents, wanting children soon, loving big shared meals, respecting elders, or all of these at once. The second: believing that a value that isn't listed doesn't exist. Someone who hasn't ticked "faith" isn't necessarily far from spirituality; they may simply not talk about it online.

The best reading is still an open question: "You put family among your values. What does that mean for you, day to day?" That one sentence is worth ten assumptions.

## Photos: what they show and what they leave out of frame

Photos are approved by moderation, and a Verified profile badge exists for people who have taken that step. It reassures you on one specific point, but says nothing beyond what is visible. A beautiful photo is no guarantee of compatibility, and an ordinary photo points to no flaw.

What you can reasonably read in a photo:

- The **setting**: a market, a beach, a kitchen, a ceremony. It suggests habits, favourite places, people around them.
- The **attitude**: a broad smile, a calm gaze, a studied pose. It shows how the person chose to present themselves, not who they are every day.
- The **details**: a book in the background, an instrument, a dish. They make excellent conversation openers.

What you can't read: character, the age of someone's heart, their life situation. And above all, you can't guess whether this person will be good for you.

## Interests: ways in, rarely proof

A list of interests is like a toolbox for starting a conversation. That's what it does best. It's far less useful as a compatibility test.

Two people who love "music" may listen to completely opposite things. Two others with no interests in common may become fascinated by each other within three messages. A shared interest is mostly valuable when it becomes a story: why this passion, since when, with whom.

That's why, when you send a Like with a short note, pointing to a specific interest, answer or photo carries more weight than a general compliment. You show that you looked, not just skimmed.

## Spotting a real point of connection

A real point of connection is a place where your life and theirs genuinely touch, even lightly. Not a surface similarity ("we both like travelling"), but a thread you can pull.

Here's how to recognise one:

1. **It's precise.** A detail, not a category.
2. **It makes you react.** You feel a small urge to reply, a smile, an anecdote rising.
3. **It can be extended.** You can ask a question about it without forcing it.
4. **It doesn't rely on a fantasy.** It rests on what is written, not on what you assume.

Example: Mariam, in Lomé, reads on a profile "I learned to bake bread with my grandmother". She doesn't write "I love bread". She writes: "Did your grandmother teach you to knead by hand, or with a recipe that's only ever passed on by word of mouth?" She has found a thread, and she pulls it gently.

### The almost empty profile: without judging

Sometimes a profile has only one photo and two lines. The temptation is to read it as a lack of interest, calculated mystery, or even bad intentions. Breathe.

The reasons for a minimal profile are many and usually mundane: lack of time, being less at ease with writing, shyness, caution, wanting to take things slowly, or an account created recently and not yet completed. Many people prefer to open up quietly, in conversation, rather than on a form.

Here's how to approach an almost empty profile:

- **Don't fill the gaps with your fears.** A blank space isn't a hidden flaw.
- **Look for the little that's there.** A photo, an interest or one sentence is enough for a first question.
- **Ask a light, open question.** "Your profile is low-key, and I like that. What would you want people to know about you first?"
- **Keep your usual caution.** An empty profile justifies neither special suspicion nor fast-tracked trust. The same safety rules apply to everyone, and reporting or blocking are always available if something makes you uncomfortable.

## The biases that distort your reading

A few traps come up often, and it's better to know them so you can pass them without falling in.

### Falling for a photo

An attractive image sets off a chain reaction: we credit the person with gentleness, humour, reliability. Without any malice, this is called projecting. The remedy: reread the profile with the photo covered. What's left that makes you want to reach out? If almost nothing, slow down: you may like an image more than a person. If you like the text too, the photo is just a bonus.

### A list of criteria that's too rigid

"I want someone with this job, this height, from this city, of this religion, this age." A clear list protects you, but a fifteen-point list also rules out people you would have adored. Separate your **non-negotiables** (two or three, the ones tied to your deepest values) from your **preferences** (the rest, which can shift when you meet a real person). Searching by criteria in the Live Crush menu is handy, but a filter is there to sort, never to decide for you.

### Confirmation bias

When we tell ourselves "they seem too perfect" or "they seem shallow", we then go looking for proof. And we always find it. Keep your hypothesis light, ready to be proved wrong.

### Similarity bias

We feel close to people who resemble us. Yet a lasting relationship also thrives on differences that broaden us. Someone from another background, with another rhythm, can surprise you in the best way.

## A three-read method

To avoid deciding in ten seconds, try three successive reads, each with a different intention.

### First read: the impression

Look at the whole profile without analysing. Let your feelings come: warmth, curiosity, indifference, unease. Make a mental note. This impression matters, but it's only a starting point, not a judgement.

### Second read: the facts

Reread, looking only at what is written or visible. Which answers? Which values? Which interests? What details in the photos? Carefully separate what you see from what you assume. "He cooks" is a fact. "He'll be a caring husband" is an assumption.

### Third read: the thread

Now look for the point of connection: a sentence, a detail, a question you'd like to ask. If you find one, send a Like with a short note that hooks onto it. If you don't, that's fine: move on, without guilt. And if you're unsure, your Daily picks may show you other profiles that speak to you more.

This method takes two minutes and saves you a lot of back and forth. Above all, it protects you from disappointment: you write to the real person, not to the one your imagination drew.

## What a profile will never tell you

However good your reading, some things stay out of a screen's reach: how you are silent together, how you argue, how you wait for a bus in the rain, how you take a no. A profile opens a conversation, and it's the conversation that teaches you the rest.

So next time a profile stops you, do three things: read slowly, find a thread, and ask a real question. The rest belongs to the person who answers you.
