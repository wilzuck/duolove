Some sentences take longer to prepare than a wedding speech. "You should sign up too" is one of them. You turn it over in the shower, rehearse it in the taxi, and when the moment comes, you end up talking about last night's match instead.

If that hesitation sounds familiar, relax: it's actually a good sign. It shows you care more about the friendship than about being right. Here's how to talk about a dating app with someone you love, without the conversation turning into a sales pitch.

## Why we hesitate (and why that's normal)

Talking about dating with a friend means talking about their private life. Behind a simple "you should try it", they may hear: "I think you're lonely", or "I don't think you know how to go about it". Nobody said that, but it's what the ear invents when the subject is sensitive.

There's also the fear of getting involved without meaning to. If I invite them and they have a bad experience, am I partly to blame? If I invite them and it goes wonderfully, do I become the third person in the story, the one they owe everything to? Neither situation is comfortable.

And finally, there's the awkwardness around money. When an invite link can earn the referrer something, you worry about looking like you're touting for business. We'll come back to this: you can be perfectly at ease with it, as long as you hide nothing.

Keep one simple idea in mind for everything that follows: **you're suggesting, not recruiting.** The difference lies in the tone, the timing and above all in what you do after they answer.

## The right moment is rarely the one when you think of it

The reflex is to raise the subject the moment the idea crosses your mind, which is often the worst time: in the middle of a family meal, just after your friend has told you about a disappointment, or by text at eleven at night.

A few better signposts:

- **When they open the door.** They say "I never meet anyone", "every evening feels the same", "my aunt wants to introduce me to her neighbour". These aren't cries for help, but they are doors left ajar.
- **When you have time.** A line thrown out as you leave, with no chance to reply, leaves your friend alone with it.
- **When it's just the two of you.** In front of other friends, a "you should" quickly sounds like a public remark.
- **Not right after a heartbreak.** If the break-up was a few days ago, offer a listening ear, not an app. It will still be there in two months.

If none of these moments comes up, wait. There's no rush, and a conversation postponed beats a conversation forced.

## The right words: talk about yourself, not them

The best phrasing starts with "I", not "you". Compare:

> "You should sign up to Crushduo, you'll see, it'll change everything."

and

> "I started using Crushduo a while back. I like the way the profiles are presented. If you ever fancy a look, I'll show you."

The first sentence contains a judgement (you need help), a promise (it'll change everything) and a disguised order. The second tells a story, makes an offer and leaves the decision to the other person. It even includes a graceful way out: "if you ever".

Some useful little phrases:

1. "I've got no opinion on what you should do, I'm just telling you what I thought of it."
2. "You're under no obligation, I just wanted you to know it exists."
3. "If you like, I can show you how the profile works, without you signing up now."

And one phrase to avoid at all costs: "Everyone's on it." It makes anyone who isn't feel ashamed, and in any case it's not yours to say.

## What you can share about your experience, and what you keep to yourself

As soon as you talk about your experience, a boundary appears: other people's. Your conversations on Crushduo involve people who aren't there to speak for themselves. Repeating their messages, showing a screenshot, imitating the way they write to get a laugh from the table, is betraying a trust that wasn't yours to give away.

What you can say, without exposing anyone:

- what you found pleasant: photos approved by moderation, the reassuring verified badge, the profile questions and the values shown on a profile that give you a first idea of someone;
- what you learnt about yourself: what you're looking for, what you won't put up with any more, the way you write a first message;
- what you found handy: the chat with its icebreakers, being able to report or block someone, Pause mode when you need a breather, Incognito mode that hides your activity status depending on your plan.

And what you keep to yourself: the first name of someone you met, what was said in a conversation, why something ended. A good test: if the person concerned walked into the room, could you carry on talking without changing your tone? If not, change the subject.

One more piece of honesty: don't sell an outcome. You don't know whether your friend will meet someone, or when. You can say the tool exists, that you think it's well made and that you enjoyed using it. Nothing more.

## Respecting a no (and really respecting it)

A no can wear many faces: "I'll think about it", "not now", "it's not my thing", a silence, a change of subject. All of them deserve the same reply: "No problem."

Then you have to stick to it. That means:

- not following up the next week "just to see if you've had a chance";
- not asking again in front of mutual friends;
- not dropping hints every time your friend mentions feeling lonely;
- not sulking, even nicely.

If your friend comes back to it on their own, great. They'll know you heard their no, and that's exactly what leaves them free to change their mind. On the other hand, someone who's been pushed sometimes ends up signing up just for some peace and quiet, then disappears. Nobody wins.

## Don't turn a gift into pressure

Referring someone is also offering something: a way in, a bit of your time, sometimes a hand choosing a photo. A gift becomes pressure the moment it creates a debt.

Here are a few ways to create that debt without meaning to: "I've signed you up, you're not going to let me down", "I spent time helping you, the least you could do is reply to people", "So, met anyone yet?" ten days later.

Here's how to avoid it: help when you're asked, not before. Don't check their activity, don't ask them to account for themselves, don't compare with your own journey. And above all, never suggest the friendship depends on their answer.

A practical rule: if you gain something when they sign up, it's up to you to stay as neutral as possible about what happens next.

## How the Crushduo referral programme works

In the app, the referral programme is built on a personal **invite link**. You share it with whoever you like, however you like. When the person signs up with that link and the conditions shown in the app are met, you receive a **reward in Crush tokens**.

Two important points, to be honest with your friends and with yourself:

- the exact conditions, what triggers the reward and how much it is, are the ones the app shows you when you open the referral page. They can change, and that page is what counts, not this article or your memory;
- the reward goes to the referrer. So don't promise your friend anything on the app's behalf.

The tokens can then be used for features that take them, for example opening a conversation from the Live Crush menu. Nothing compulsory, nothing hidden: it's a small thank-you for spreading the word about the app.

## Present it honestly, with no staging

In practice, transparency fits in one sentence: "If you sign up with my link, I get a few tokens." Said simply, it defuses any suspicion. Your friend knows where they stand and can decide with full knowledge.

And if you're afraid it will look self-interested, you have two sincere options:

1. **Tell the truth.** "There's a referral link, I'll send it to you, but you can also sign up without it." Nobody will hold it against you for being clear.
2. **Don't use the link at all.** That's your right, and nobody will know. There's no merit and no fault either way.

Above all, avoid made-up scenarios: the fake coincidence ("Oh look, I just happen to have this app"), the polished testimonial, the story post where everything looks magical. Your friend will spot the staging a mile off, and that, more than the app, is what will damage their trust.

## After the invitation: be there without hovering

If your friend signs up, your role almost ends there. You can offer to take a look at their profile, because an outside eye on the photo or bio is sometimes useful. You can explain reporting and blocking in case they come across behaviour that makes them uncomfortable. You can mention that Pause mode exists if it all gets too much.

But you don't need to ask for news every evening, or know about their conversations. A good referrer is like a road sign: useful when you're looking for it, discreet the rest of the time.

Telling a friend about Crushduo means showing them a door, not pushing them through it. Pick a calm moment, talk about what you liked, leave a clear way out, hear the no once, and say plainly that a referral link can earn you tokens. If, after all that, your friend says "thanks, but it's not for me", you'll have kept what matters most: the friendship, intact, and one more conversation where everyone felt respected.
