You've met someone good. The problem: they live five hours' drive away, in another city in the country, sometimes in another country altogether. Cotonou and Parakou, Abidjan and Bouaké, Lomé and Kara, Dakar and Saint-Louis: these journeys, which many families already make for other reasons, suddenly become the centre of your love life.

A relationship between two cities is far from impossible, but it needs organisation that love at first sight doesn't provide. Here's how to make it workable without wearing yourself out or going broke.

## Start by being clear

The distance should be **put on the table** from the start, not discovered in the fourth conversation:

- Tell each other **where** you live, and whether either of you could one day consider moving.
- Be clear about **what you're looking for**: a lasting long-distance relationship, or a plan to move closer eventually?
- Talk about **budget and time**: how often can you realistically see each other? What does your schedule look like?

This frankness avoids disappointment. It also lets you check that you share the same vision: someone who sees the distance as temporary and someone who sees it as permanent won't last long together.

## Budget: a subject to tackle without embarrassment

Travel is expensive, especially when it's repeated. Rather than letting the subject poison the relationship, raise it:

- **Estimate the cost of a visit** (transport, accommodation, meals) and decide together how to share it.
- **Take turns**: one weekend at one person's place, the next at the other's, so the effort is balanced.
- **Don't make the relationship depend on a financial gesture.** Paying for the other person's travel must be a free choice, never a condition. And the safety rule still applies until you've met: **no money sent to someone you haven't seen in real life**.

## Meet early, so you know

The most common mistake is staying for weeks, even months, in a purely virtual relationship. The more attachment grows at a distance, the more is riding on the real meeting, and the greater the risk of disappointment.

- **Aim for a first meeting quickly**, within the first few weeks if you feel confident.
- **Choose a public place** the first time, and let someone you trust know what you're doing.
- **Keep the first visit short**: a day, or a weekend. You'll soon see whether being together in person confirms what the messages promised.

## Between visits: keeping the connection

The real difficulty is everyday life between meetings. A few habits that help:

- **A regular rhythm, not an overwhelming one.** A short message in the morning, a call in the evening, and silence in between. Talking twenty times a day ends up suffocating.
- **Rituals of your own.** A film watched together, each on your own side, a song shared on Fridays, a question of the day, a photo from your day.
- **Short but attentive calls.** Ten minutes of real listening beat an hour of distracted conversation.
- **Things to look forward to.** Talk about what's ahead: a plan, an anecdote, an outing. Avoid only telling each other about your problems.
- **Accepted silences.** A busy day isn't a loss of feeling. Agree together on what's normal in your rhythm.

## Handling fear and jealousy

Distance feeds the imagination: "What's he doing tonight? Who with?" These thoughts are human, but they get worse if you keep them to yourself.

- **Talk about your fears without accusing.** "I had a moment of doubt tonight, it helps me to talk about it" is better than "Why didn't you reply?"
- **Define your expectations** about faithfulness, going out, social media. What isn't said gets interpreted.
- **Look at the facts.** Reliable behaviour over time is more soothing than promises.
- **Don't monitor.** Snooping, questioning and checking never reassure for long: they damage trust.

## Give it a horizon

A long-distance relationship needs a direction. Without a plan to move closer, it becomes a tiring habit. Without pressure, but without avoiding it, talk about it:

- Who could move for work, and by when?
- What are the conditions for moving closer (job, family, studies)?
- What happens if it isn't possible within a year? This question is uncomfortable, but it protects both of you.

Setting a **review date** (for example six months) gives you a framework: you take stock together, honestly, of what's going well and what needs to change.

## When families get involved

In many families, distance is a worry: "She lives so far away, how will that work?" A few tips:

- **Introduce the person gradually**, once the relationship is solid, and prepare for that meeting.
- **Explain your plan** rather than defending yourself: "We see each other every month, and we have a plan to move closer."
- **Respect the customs** that matter to your loved ones, without giving up deciding for yourself.

## Small practical details that make everything easier

A long-distance relationship also depends on day-to-day organisation:

- **Plan your journeys ahead.** Book early, avoid travelling at night if possible, and let someone close to you know your arrival time.
- **Choose the accommodation together**, clearly: who's hosting, where you'll sleep, what's been agreed. Misunderstandings often grow out of what hasn't been said.
- **Build in safety margins**: a power bank, a backup connection, a plan B if transport is cancelled.
- **Think about the return trip**: a weekend that ends in a rush spoils the good moments. Plan a comfortable departure time.
- **Keep time for yourself**: a successful visit doesn't replace your friends, your work and your rest.

These details seem trivial, but they're what makes a visit leave a good memory, or a tiredness you carry for a long time.

## Knowing when to stop

Sometimes, despite good will, distance wins: exhaustion, disagreement about the future, no way to move closer. In that case, it's better to end things respectfully than to get bogged down. Say things clearly, give thanks for what was beautiful, and give each other time to recover.

> A long-distance relationship isn't measured in kilometres, but in the quality of what you build between journeys.

If you're lucky enough to meet someone worth the kilometres, give yourselves the means to find out: meet early, talk clearly, keep a sustainable rhythm and set a horizon. The rest is the road.
