The last message is yours. It was sent on Tuesday. Two grey ticks, then nothing. You put the phone down, went back to the washing-up, and opened the app three more times before dinner. What makes silence so cruel is that it says nothing, and the mind fills the gap with the worst it can find.

This article is about both sides of the door: the person left without a reply, and the person who no longer feels like replying. Because almost everyone has been both, at different moments in their life.

## Why people disappear: rarely glorious reasons, rarely cruel ones

It is tempting to imagine a verdict: "He found me boring." The reality is usually more ordinary and more scattered.

- **Life took over.** A work week that spilled over, a cousin arriving to stay, a bereavement, plain exhaustion. A message waiting for a reply "when I have the energy" sometimes ends up buried under the next ones.
- **Doubt became awkward.** Someone senses the spark has gone, does not know how to say it without hurting, and chooses to dodge. It is not pretty, but it is human.
- **Several conversations at once.** When you talk to several people at the same time, some get dropped through disorganisation as much as by choice.
- **Fear of confrontation.** Saying no directly, even in writing, takes a courage some people have not learnt yet.
- **Real discomfort.** A question that came too fast, a tone that slipped, a topic the other person did not like. Silence is then a clumsy answer.

None of these reasons is proof of your worth. They speak about the person going quiet, their schedule, their courage, their state of mind at the time. That does not make them any nicer to live through, but it changes the story you tell yourself.

## What silence does not say

It does not say you were boring. It does not say your photo was bad, your job off-putting or your accent a problem. Nor does it say the other person is a monster.

It says one thing, and one useful thing only: right now, this person is not replying to you. That is all. The rest is fiction written by fear. When you catch a thought like "it must be because of me", put it on the table the way you would put down an overripe fruit, and look at it: is there a single fact behind it?

## Three sentences to end things cleanly

If you are the one who no longer wants to continue, you do not owe a lecture. Three honest sentences are enough, and they fit in a ten-second message.

1. **Say thank you.** "Thank you for our chats, I enjoyed talking with you."
2. **Tell the truth, without the details.** "I feel we are not heading in the same direction, and I would rather tell you straight."
3. **Close gently.** "I wish you some lovely encounters."

There is no false hope in there, no "let's talk soon" you will not keep, no justification that opens a debate. Notice what is left out, too: no "you are too this" or "I deserve better". You talk about yourself.

A few variations, depending on the moment:

- After three days of chatting: "I would rather be honest, I don't feel a spark. Thank you for your kindness, all the best to you."
- After a few weeks: "I've realised my heart isn't in it, and it wouldn't be fair to keep you waiting. Thank you for everything we shared."
- When you are simply running on empty: "I'm going through a period where I can't really invest myself. I'd rather say so than disappear."

If the message is met with anger or pressure, you do not have to argue. You said what needed saying. That is enough.

## Follow up or let go: a simple, gentle rule

Should you follow up? The answer comes down to two things: how long the silence has lasted, and what kind of silence it is.

### One follow-up, and only one

If the conversation was warm and the silence has lasted several days, one light message can make sense. No reproach, no "did you read this?". Rather, a sentence that feels like a small gesture: "Hello, I hope your week is going well. If you'd rather leave it there, no problem at all, just let me know."

This message has a rare quality: it leaves an honourable way out. It gives the other person the chance to reply without shame, and gives you the chance to close the door.

### After that, let go

If nothing comes after that follow-up, that is the answer. No need for a second, a third, or an angry message at midnight. Each extra follow-up turns a silence into tension, and leaves you with less dignity than you had when you started.

One way to decide: "If I were in their place, how would I feel receiving this message?" If the answer is "cornered", it is one message too many.

## Don't let your self-esteem pay the bill

This is the most important part. You cannot negotiate with a silence, but what you do with it belongs to you.

A few concrete, ordinary things that genuinely help.

- **Take the phone out of the room.** Not out of discipline, simply because refreshing it every ten minutes keeps the wound open.
- **Fill your evening.** Cook something you love, walk to the end of the street, call a friend who makes you laugh, go to the family gathering or meal without thinking about the screen.
- **Name the hurt.** "I'm disappointed, and that's normal." Disappointment you acknowledge weighs less than disappointment you hide.
- **Look for the facts.** What do I actually know? Only that they have not replied. The rest is a story.
- **Remember what is yours.** Your worth does not depend on one conversation. It was there before, and it will be there after.

You can also use the app to protect yourself. Quiet hours for notifications let you cut out the noise at certain times, for example in the evening, when worry talks the loudest. And if it all starts to feel too heavy, Pause mode is there: you stop, you breathe, you come back when you decide to.

Finally, bring some gentleness into your next step. You do not have to start chatting with a new profile within the hour. Letting a day or two pass is not a weakness.

## When silence is no longer the problem: pressure, insults, harassment

There is another, very different situation: when someone replies too much. Messages that keep coming after a no, reproaches, insults, repeated demands to carry on, inappropriate remarks, threats. Here the aim is no longer to end things "gently", it is to protect yourself.

The rule is clear: **you owe no explanation to someone who does not respect your no.**

- **Blocking** ends the conversation without discussion. It is not a hostile act, it is a tool, and you have the right to use it as soon as you feel uncomfortable, without waiting for proof.
- **Reporting** alerts the moderation team so they can look into the situation. Describe what happened in simple words, and keep the messages concerned as they are.
- **Incognito mode**, for its part, hides your activity status if you want to keep a lower profile for a while.

If you feel genuinely in danger, talk to someone you trust close to you and, depending on how serious it is, to the relevant authorities. This article does not replace their help.

And if you are the one who has been blocked: do not try to get around it. A block is an answer.

## When you are the one who disappeared

One last word for those who have already left a message unanswered, which is almost everyone. It does not make you a bad person. But there is a way to make up for it: send, even late, the three sentences above. "I'm sorry I didn't reply. I wasn't clear enough with myself. I wish you the very best." It takes a minute, and it gives the other person the closure they were waiting for.

Think, too, about what you would like to receive, and give it: a simple answer is better than a long absence.

## For next time

A long silence is an answer without words, and you have the right to take it as it is. Follow up once, lightly, or not at all. If you are leaving, leave with three honest sentences. If you are the one left, protect your evenings and what you think of yourself. And if someone crosses a line, block and report, without justifying yourself. The next conversation deserves a rested heart.
