A photo shows what you look like. A bio says what you are looking for. Profile questions, on the other hand, hint at **how you think**. They are short sentences to complete or questions to answer in a few words, and that is often where people stop, smile and send a message.

Yet many members leave them empty or answer in two words. Here is how to make them a real asset, without turning your profile into an essay.

## Why they work

A specific question is easier to comment on than a general introduction. When someone reads "My comfort food after a long day: rice with peanut sauce, and I don't share", they immediately have a thousand ways to react. They can joke, share theirs, ask you for the recipe. Your answer **makes the first move for you**.

It is also where you can show sides of yourself a photo cannot: humour, tenderness, self-mockery, curiosity.

## How many questions, and which ones?

You can answer several questions. It is better to pick **three or four answered well** than five rushed ones. Choose the ones where you genuinely have something to say:

- One about **your everyday life** (a ritual, a habit, a quirk).
- One about **your tastes** (a song, a dish, a place).
- One about **what you are looking for** or **what matters to you**.
- Possibly a **lighter or funny** one, to relax the tone.

Avoid making them all serious, or all jokey: balance gives the impression of a whole person.

## Writing an answer that hooks: the small-detail rule

A good answer is one or two sentences long and contains **one concrete detail**. Compare:

- "I like music." → tells us nothing.
- "I know every Angélique Kidjo album by heart, and I sing off-key with total conviction." → a person appears.

The concrete detail can be a name, a place, a habit, a number, a scene:

- Instead of "I like travelling", write "My record: three cities in one weekend, one bag, zero plan."
- Instead of "I'm a family person", write "At my place on Sundays, there's always room for one more at the table."

## Clichés to avoid

Some answers come up so often that they no longer catch anyone's eye:

- "I'm a simple person."
- "I'm looking for my soulmate."
- "I like laughing, going out, travelling."
- "No drama."

They are not lies, but they say nothing. When you are tempted to write one, ask yourself: **what do I actually do that shows this?** If you are simple, what do you do? If you love to laugh, what makes you laugh? Write the answer to that second question.

## Think of the person reading

Your answer is read by someone hesitating between two profiles. It should make their life easier:

- **Short**: one or two sentences, readable at a glance on a phone.
- **Open**: it leaves something to bounce off rather than closing the subject.
- **Warm**: even humour works better when it invites rather than crushes.

A good test: imagine someone replying to you. What would they say? If you already have three ideas for replies, you have nailed it. If you can't see any, rework your sentence.

## What you can keep for later

Profile questions are not a confessional. You don't have to talk about:

- your romantic wounds;
- your financial situation;
- health problems or family details you don't want to hand over to strangers;
- information that could pinpoint where you are (neighbourhood, workplace, schedule).

A good rule: **put nothing there you wouldn't say to a stranger met in a waiting room**. Trust is built in stages, and these subjects will find their place in conversation, when you decide.

## Using them to reply to others

Other people's profile questions are also a goldmine for your first message. A specific answer is a ready-made way in: you can quote a detail, ask a question, share something in common. It works far better than a "Hi, how's it going?". When you send a Like on a specific answer rather than on a whole profile, your little comment makes sense straight away, and if you become a duo, it becomes the start of your conversation.

## Three ways to spoil a good answer

Even with good intentions, it is easy to fall into the same traps:

- **The mirror answer.** You copy what everyone else writes: "My greatest quality: my honesty." Nobody admits to being a liar, so the sentence sets nobody apart. Instead, tell a story about a time your honesty cost you something.
- **The barrier answer.** "What I can't stand: liars, cheats, people who are late." You think you are protecting yourself; mostly you come across as suspicious. Rephrase it positively: what you appreciate in someone.
- **The riddle answer.** "I'm hard to describe." That is a way of not answering. A partial but precise answer is better than artificial mystery.

When in doubt, read your answer out loud. If it sounds like it came from an official form, start again as if you were writing to a friend.

## A word on humour

Humour is attractive, as long as it stays **inclusive**. Joking about yourself (your flaws, your quirks, your failed cooking) creates closeness. Joking about other people, about a group or using stereotypes can close doors without you ever knowing. A simple rule: if a joke needs the other person to be "in on it" not to be offended, it has no place on a profile read by strangers.

## Keep them up to date

Your life changes, and so should your answers. Reread them from time to time: an answer that no longer sounds like you makes a poor impression on a first date ("You wrote that you go running every morning..."). Changing an answer takes a minute, and a living profile gives the impression of a person who is present.

## An example of a balanced set

- **My Sunday ritual**: "Market in the morning, compulsory nap in the afternoon, a call to my grandmother in the evening."
- **My playlist right now**: "A mix of rumba, Afrobeats, and jazz when it rains."
- **What I'm looking for**: "Someone I can talk to about anything, and be quiet with, comfortably."

Three answers, a few words each, and already a recognisable person. None of them is spectacular, but they are all true and alive. That is exactly what you need: to make the other person want to finish the sentence.
