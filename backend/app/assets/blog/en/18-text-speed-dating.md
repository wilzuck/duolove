Picture a room where everyone changes chairs every few minutes, but nobody speaks. You write. Sentences arrive, you reply, a small signal tells you it's time to move on to the next person, and only at the very end do you say who you'd like to see again.

That, roughly, is the spirit of text speed dating on Crushduo. No face to hold eye contact with while you search for words, no awkward silence to fill, no noisy room. Just sentences, rhythm and a decision. Here is how to approach it calmly, even if the idea makes you sweat a little.

## What a session looks like

Sessions are **published by the Crushduo team**: you don't organise them yourself, you spot them and **sign up** for the one that suits you. When the time comes, the idea is simple:

- you chat in writing with one person, then another, over a series of **short rotations**;
- each conversation stays light by nature: there's no time to tell your whole story;
- at the **end of the session**, you make your **decisions**: who you'd like to get back in touch with, and who not.

The precise details (time, format, what is shown for each session) are on the session page itself. So as not to mislead you, this article gives no durations or numbers: the team displays them, and theirs is the version that counts.

This format changes one essential thing: you aren't building a relationship, you're having **several small encounters the size of a sketch**. A sketch doesn't need to be perfect for you to recognise someone.

## Preparing in five minutes

No need to revise. But five well-spent minutes take away half the stress.

### Minute one: glance at your profile

The people you'll meet will see your profile. Reread it as a stranger would: is the photo (approved by moderation) recent? Do your values and your answers to the profile questions say something about you? The verified badge also reassures others, if you have it.

### Minute two: pick three topics to keep handy

Not a script, just three small stories or preferences you can bring out effortlessly: a dish you never tire of, a place you go when you need calm, something you learned recently. They'll be your lifebuoys if the conversation stalls.

### Minute three: prepare an opening line

One opening line, just one, that isn't "hi, how are you?". For example: "If you had a free hour right now, what would you do with it?" It works because it calls for a concrete answer, not a polite hello.

### Minute four: sort out your surroundings

A place without interruptions, a charged phone, notifications from other apps switched off. If you want more peace of mind, think about the privacy options the app already offers, such as Incognito mode, which hides your activity status depending on your plan.

### Minute five: set an intention

Not "I have to find someone". Rather: "I'm going to be curious about three people." A modest intention holds up better than a huge expectation.

## Questions that make a real exchange

In a short time, the classic questions (job, age, city) quickly feel like filling in a form. Good questions have three qualities: they can be answered in one or two sentences, they tell you something, and they leave room for the other person.

A few ideas:

1. **Small everyday things.** "What's your favourite way to spend a Sunday with no plans?"
2. **Preferences that reveal character.** "Are you the type to arrive early, or to dash in at the last minute?"
3. **Values, without solemnity.** "What were you taught as a child that you still do today?"
4. **Curiosities.** "What surprised you this week?"
5. **Follow-ups.** Nothing beats a simple "Tell me a bit more" when something catches your attention.

And a few questions to keep at the bottom of your pocket: anything that sounds like an interrogation ("why are you single?"), a test ("do you want children?" in the first minute) or an investigation into the past. There will be a time for serious subjects, but it isn't during a sketch.

Another reflex: answer your own questions. Asking "and you, what's your favourite dish?" after sharing yours makes an exchange. Asking ten questions without ever revealing anything makes an inspection.

## Handling awkwardness

There is almost always a moment when awkwardness shows up. The other person replies in one word, you don't know what to say any more, or on the contrary they write three paragraphs and you're lost. None of these is a failure.

- **If you go blank:** go back to one of your three lifebuoys, or ask a simple question about what was just said. Picking up a detail from their answer is always a good idea.
- **If the other person replies curtly:** don't read it as rejection. Some people write little when they're nervous. A touch of humour or a more open question may be enough.
- **If something strikes you as clumsy:** let it go. One awkward sentence doesn't say who someone is. You don't have to pick up on everything.
- **If an exchange makes you uncomfortable:** you can stop, and if necessary use the report or block options. Your comfort comes before politeness.

One sentence you can allow yourself: "I'm a bit nervous, it's my first one!" It almost always raises a smile on the other side, and it relaxes the person who says it too.

## When time is short

The pace is exactly what makes this format different. The time limit forces you to write briefly, get to the point and leave the long introductions for wedding receptions.

A few habits help:

- write short sentences, even if it means sending two messages instead of one big block;
- don't look for the perfect answer: a true answer beats a brilliant one that arrives too late;
- if a topic really grabs you and time runs out, say so: "I'd have loved to keep going on that". It's valuable information, for you and for them, when it's time to decide;
- accept leaving a question unanswered. It isn't an oversight, it's the format.

Time pressure has a secret advantage: it stops you putting on a show. It's hard to play a character across several rotations. You end up writing as you are.

## Deciding without judging yourself

At the end, you make your choices. This is often when the inner critic arrives: "I'm too picky", "I'm not open enough", "why didn't I choose that one, they were nice?"

A few gentler ways to go about it:

1. **Trust the feeling, not the list.** After each chat, note in one word how you felt: "light", "tense", "curious", "nothing". That sticks better than arguments.
2. **Tell "nice" apart from "want to continue".** You can like someone without wanting to get back in touch. The two don't contradict each other.
3. **Choose broadly or narrowly, but without shame.** Some people keep three names, others one, others none. Saying no to everyone is a valid decision.
4. **Don't compare yourself with the other participants.** You won't know what they felt, and it doesn't matter.
5. **Remember that your choice isn't a verdict.** Saying "not for me" doesn't judge the person; it only says the sketch didn't make you want to finish the painting.

And if you hesitate, ask yourself: "Would I like to read the next message from this person tomorrow morning?" The answer often arrives before the thinking does.

## What to do with mutual choices

When two people have chosen each other at the end of the session, the door opens a little: you can pick up the conversation again, this time with no stopwatch. The simplest thing is to get back to it quickly, while the exchange is still fresh.

A few ideas for this second stage:

- start by recalling a detail from the session: "Your story about Sundays with no plans stayed with me";
- use the messaging at your own pace, with the icebreakers or reactions if they help you get started;
- let the conversation stretch out, without trying to catch up on everything the format didn't allow;
- if you feel like it, suggest a voice or video call, which is available between the two people in an active duo, before thinking about meeting;
- and if, on reflection, you feel the spark isn't there any more, nothing obliges you: you can end the exchange simply.

For choices that aren't mutual, there's nothing more to do than carry on your way. It isn't a failure: it's how a format works when two wishes have to meet.

## Before the next session

One last piece of advice: don't judge a session by its result. A session where you kept nobody may be the one where you finally understood what kind of conversation makes you want to write. A session where you laughed a lot is already worth something.

Note the questions that worked well, the ones that led to silence, and what you'd like to try next time. Then, when the team publishes a new session, sign up only if you feel like it. Text speed dating is a format for people who enjoy short, genuine exchanges, not a test to pass.
