Ask the people around you what attracts them to someone and you'll often hear the same answer: "Someone who makes me laugh", "Someone with a sense of humour". Laughter relaxes, brings people closer and reveals a lot about a person: their quick wit, their kindness, the way they see the world.

But humour is also slippery ground. A joke that lands well among friends can hurt someone you barely know. This guide helps you use humour as a bridge, never as a weapon, in your messages as well as on a date.

## Why humour is attractive

Humour sends several messages at once:

- **"I'm at ease."** Someone who jokes around seems relaxed, which puts the other person at ease too.
- **"I'm paying attention to you."** Picking up on what the other person just said with a witty reply proves you're listening.
- **"We could get on well."** Laughing at the same things is a sign of connection.
- **"I don't take myself too seriously."** A much-appreciated quality, especially when you're looking for something lasting.

You don't need to be a professional comedian. Nobody's asking you to put on a show, just to bring a little lightness.

## Self-deprecation, a safe bet

Laughing at yourself is often the safest kind of humour, as long as you keep it in proportion.

- **It defuses nerves**: "Fair warning: I can't dance without stepping on someone's toes."
- **It shows humility** without putting yourself down.
- **It invites the other person to open up** in return.

Be careful, though, not to slide into constant self-criticism. "I'm hopeless in the kitchen, my rice always ends up as one big lump" raises a smile. "Anyway, nobody will ever want me" makes people uncomfortable. **Self-deprecation should stay light and occasional.**

## Topics to avoid

Some jokes hurt even when there's no bad intention behind them. Early on, it's best to stay well away from:

- **Appearance.** Weight, height, skin colour, hair, teeth: even as teasing, these remarks often touch sensitive spots.
- **Origin and ethnicity.** Clichés about a particular region, country or community may seem harmless, but they reduce a person to a label.
- **Religion.** Faith is personal and important to many people. Joking about someone's practices or beliefs rarely goes down well.
- **Family or financial situation.** Divorce, children, unemployment, housing: all subjects to handle with care.
- **Sexist jokes** that box men or women into caricatured roles.

The rule is simple: **if the joke targets who the other person is, rather than a situation you share, leave it out.**

## Humour that brings you closer

A few playgrounds that are almost risk-free:

- **Everyday situations**: the traffic jams, the power cut at the worst possible moment, the neighbour blasting music on a Sunday morning.
- **Life's little contradictions**: "I worked out this morning. Well, I watched a workout video."
- **Shared references**: a series, a film or a song you both love.
- **Nods to your conversation**: picking up an anecdote the other person told you and turning it into a shared joke, without ever mocking them.

## Humour in writing: the traps to avoid

In writing, you lose the tone of voice, the smile, the look in someone's eyes. An ironic line can be taken literally and cause offence without you ever knowing.

- **Go easy on irony and heavy sarcasm** until you know the person well.
- **Use emojis in moderation.** A smile or a wink can clear up an ambiguity, but too many emojis muddy the message.
- **Reread before you send.** Ask yourself: "If someone wrote this to me, how would I take it?"
- **Don't answer everything with a joke.** If the other person shares something personal, humour can make it look as if you're dodging.
- **If a message falls flat, just say so**: "I don't think my joke landed. What I meant was..."

To start a conversation, the **icebreakers** and the **first-message coach** draw on the other person's profile: their **interests** and their answers to the **profile questions** are often a great basis for a personalised touch of humour.

## Showing your humour on your profile

Your **bio** is the first place people discover your personality. A light line can make all the difference:

- "Self-proclaimed champion of Sunday alloco (fried plantain). Open to challengers."
- "Looking for someone to argue with about who makes the best jollof. Hint: it's me."

Keep a balance: a touch of humour, then a few sincere lines about what you're looking for. A bio made up entirely of jokes leaves the other person guessing who you really are.

## Knowing when to be serious too

Humour is attractive, but it isn't enough to build a serious relationship. There are moments when lightness has no place:

- **When the other person talks about their wounds**, their family, their worries.
- **When you discuss values**, life plans, faith, children.
- **When a misunderstanding sets in**: better to clear it up than to joke about it.

Someone who knows how to laugh and how to listen inspires trust. **It's the balance between the two that reassures.**

## When the other person's humour bothers you

If the other person makes a joke that hurts you, you have every right to say so calmly: "I know you meant it as a joke, but that subject is sensitive for me." Their reaction will tell you a lot. A respectful person apologises and adjusts. If the hurtful remarks continue, you can end the conversation, **block** them and, if necessary, **report** the profile.

> The best humour makes both people laugh, not one at the other's expense.

So dare to be light-hearted. Laugh at yourself, at the little things in everyday life, at the coincidences in your conversation. Stay alert to how the other person reacts, adjust if needed, and don't forget to be sincere when the moment calls for it. Some of the best connections begin with a shared burst of laughter.
