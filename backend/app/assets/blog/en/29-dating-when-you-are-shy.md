Shy people hear it all the time: "You just have to go for it!" As if shyness were a lack of willpower, something you could simply push through and leave behind. But a reserved person isn't someone running behind everyone else. They're simply more attentive, slower to open up, more aware of how others see them. Those are precious qualities, in love as much as anywhere.

Dating apps can actually be a gift for shy people: you write before you meet, you choose your words, you move forward step by step. Here's how to make the most of that without forcing yourself.

## What shyness is not

Before talking about method, let's clear away a few myths:

- **It isn't indifference.** A shy person is often very interested, but shows it little, for fear of getting it wrong.
- **It isn't a lack of character.** Many shy people have strong convictions, a quiet sense of humour and deep loyalty.
- **It isn't a permanent barrier.** It eases over time, through small experiences that go well.

So the goal isn't to "become an extrovert", but to find ways of meeting people that respect your temperament.

## Writing your profile when you hate blowing your own trumpet

Shyness often makes writing a profile painful: it feels like selling yourself. A few pointers:

- **Talk about what you do, not what you're worth.** "On Sundays I sketch in a notebook I never show anyone" says more than any flattering adjective.
- **Choose photos where you feel comfortable**, not portraits where you're playing a role. A photo taken while doing something you enjoy is often more natural than a posed shot.
- **Use the profile questions**, which give you a structure: answering a question is easier than filling a blank page.
- **Just say it**: "Quiet at first, very chatty once we know each other." That one line heads off the assumption that you're not interested.

## First messages: small steps

For a shy person, the first message is often the hardest part. Rather than aiming for the perfect message, aim for one that's **easy to send**:

- **Pick up on a detail from their profile**: "I saw you like jazz, do you have a favourite album?"
- **Send a Like with a short note**, which becomes your first message if you make a duo: one sentence is enough, and you only have to write it once.
- **Prepare two or three opening lines** you can adapt. That isn't cheating: it's preparation, like before a presentation.
- **Ask an open question**, so you don't have to carry the whole conversation.

Written conversations are a real advantage: you have time to think about what you say, which many spontaneous people don't.

## Coping with silences and slow replies

Silence is the shy person's worst ordeal, because it instantly reads as rejection. A few useful reminders:

- **A slow reply is almost never a message.** Work, tiredness and busy days explain most silences.
- **Don't write ten times.** A single follow-up, two or three days later, is enough. After that, let it be or close the conversation.
- **Keep yourself busy.** Close the app once you've replied. The more you look at it, the more you wait.
- **Don't treat a silence as a verdict on your worth.** It tells you about the other person's availability, not about you.

## Moving from text to voice

This is often the most daunting step. A gentle way to take it:

1. **Mention it in advance**: "I'd love to hear your voice one of these days, a quick ten-minute call, would you be up for it?" A heads-up takes away the surprise.
2. **Suggest a short, specific moment**, a weekday evening, not a long, vague window.
3. **Choose audio** if video makes you freeze: a voice is enough to build a connection.
4. **Prepare three topics** (a story, a question, a plan) so you don't dread the pauses.
5. **Accept the silences.** Ten seconds of silence feels like a minute to you, and it's rarely a problem for the other person.

## The first date: lowering the pressure

A few adjustments help you stay in control:

- **Choose a place you know**: fewer unknowns to deal with.
- **Suggest an activity rather than sitting face to face**: a walk, a market, a coffee on a terrace. Attention is shared between the two of you and your surroundings.
- **Plan something short**: an hour is plenty for a first time, and you can always suggest staying longer if it's going well.
- **Arrive a little early**, to settle in and breathe.
- **Say so if you're nervous.** "I'm a bit stressed, I'm not great at introductions with people I don't know": admitting it often relaxes both of you.

## Duo Nights and speed dating: reassuring formats

Contrary to what you might think, structured formats often suit shy people well:

- During an online **Duo Night**, everyone is connected for the same reason at the same time: nobody has to explain why they're there.
- In **text-based speed dating**, the exchanges are short and structured, and you write at your own pace.

In these formats, the setting carries the conversation, not your charisma.

## Don't judge yourself harshly

Every experience is a lesson. A clumsy message, a conversation that fizzles out, a slightly awkward date: it's all part of the journey, and most of the people you write to have felt the same at some point. Note what went well, not just what went badly. Set yourself one small challenge a week (a Like with a note, a slightly longer reply, a suggested call), not ten.

> Shyness doesn't stop you being loved. It only asks you to meet people who know how to wait.

And those people exist: people who value gentleness, listening and sincerity. They aren't always the first to write, but they're often the ones who stay.
