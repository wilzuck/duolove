It's nine in the evening, the power has just come back on, and your battery is at forty per cent. You open a profile you like. Your thumb hovers over the keyboard. Two letters come out almost on their own: "Hi". Then "how are you?". Sent. You put the phone down and go and eat. The next day, nothing.

It's not that you're uninteresting. It's that this message gives the other person nothing to hold on to.

## The "hi, how are you?" that fades without a sound

Think about the person receiving it. Their day is already full: the family group chat, the seller following up, the colleague who needs a file, and somewhere in the middle, your "hi, how are you?". To answer it, they have to invent the rest of the conversation for you. Replying "fine, and you?" starts a game of table tennis where each side sends the ball back without ever really playing it.

Most of the time, the person isn't turning you down. They put it off until later, and "later" ends up buried under notifications. The silence isn't a judgement on you. It's the result of a message that didn't ask for anything specific.

A good first message does the opposite: it makes replying easy. It contains a detail you noticed, and a door left ajar, a question that can be answered in one sentence without giving too much away.

## What the other person actually sees

They see your name, your photo and three lines of text. In a few seconds, they decide whether to take the time or not. What stops them isn't clumsiness: a slightly awkward but specific sentence goes down very well. What stops them is an anonymous message, the kind that could have been sent to ten other people that same evening.

A simple test before you hit send: could this message be copied and pasted to someone else? If so, it isn't ready yet. It's missing a sign of presence, proof that you looked at this particular profile and not another one.

## Read a profile the way you'd shop at a market

At the market, a good shopper doesn't grab the first tomato in the pile. She looks, she touches, she spots what stands out: the heavier mango, the stall where you can smell fresh chilli. Do the same with a profile. Don't skim it, explore it.

On Crushduo, a profile gives you three raw materials: answers to the personality questions, values, and photos. Each one offers a different kind of hook.

## Three ways to hook a conversation

### Personality answers

This is the gold mine, because these answers are written in the person's own words. Someone who says they can never resist a plate of their aunt's riz gras, a rich, oily jollof-style rice, hands you twenty topics at once: the aunt, the party, the sauce, the never-ending argument about the right recipe. Pick the answer that made you smile or that you disagree with, and bounce off it.

### Values

They are more serious, so they come up less often in first messages, and that's exactly why they stand out. If someone says family or faith matters to them, don't congratulate them: ask how it plays out day to day. "At your place, is Sunday more everyone round the table, or everyone on their own schedule?" shows that you read their profile and that you're curious about the real life behind the word.

### Photos

Here's the trap: commenting on looks. "You're gorgeous" is kind, but it's a line they've already read a hundred times. Look at what's around them instead: the setting, an object, a gesture. A market in the background, a football shirt, a dish placed in front of the camera, a motorbike on a red dirt road. A detail from the background proves you actually looked, and it starts a story.

## Before and after: five messages rewritten

The names below are made up, so you can see the difference in practice.

**Before:** "Hi Aminata, how are you?"

**After:** "Aminata, your answer about phone-free Sundays made me laugh. Do you really last the whole day, or does the network win by around three o'clock?"

**Before:** "Good evening Koffi, you're very handsome."

**After:** "Koffi, is the third photo a neighbourhood football match? I want to know who won, and whether you were the one arguing with the referee."

**Before:** "Hey, can we chat?"

**After:** "Good evening Prisca. You wrote that you love cooking for others more than for yourself. What's the dish everyone always asks you for?"

**Before:** "Hello Serge, what do you do for a living?"

**After:** "Serge, you said you value patience. I'm terrible at it: tell me your trick for not losing your temper in morning traffic."

**Before:** "Hello Fatou, I'd like to get to know you."

**After:** "Fatou, it looks like we feel the same way about tea: very sweet or not at all, never in between. Which side are you on?"

What they have in common: a precise detail, a light tone, an easy question. None of them is brilliant, and all of them can be sent in thirty seconds. Notice too that none of them is trying to impress. They invite.

## The short note attached to a Like

A Like on its own is a quiet signal: "I saw you." It's nice, but it doesn't say why. With a short note, you give the reason, and that's what the person reads first.

The note follows the same rules as a first message, only shorter: one line, one detail, no heavy question. "Your answer about tea won me over, I want to hear the rest" beats three paragraphs. It arrives at the very moment the person is deciding whether your profile deserves a look: your sentence tips the balance.

For the times when you really want to be noticed, there's also the Super Like. Save it for a profile that genuinely moves you, not for ten of them: its value comes from being rare. And if you simply want more people to discover your own profile, that's what the Boost is for, though it's no substitute for a well-written message.

## When silence is the answer

You sent a lovely message, and nothing came back. Breathe. There are so many possible reasons that treating silence as a verdict means misreading it: the person may be travelling, with family, in the middle of the festive season, or simply tired of writing to strangers.

A few simple steps:

1. **Wait** two or three days, not two hours. A second message sent too quickly feels like pressure.
2. **Follow up once only**, with something new, never a "are you there?". For example: "I was thinking about your phone-free Sundays, I made it to midday."
3. **Then let it go.** A message left unanswered isn't a failure, it's information. There are other profiles and other evenings.
4. **Change the setting.** If text alone isn't working, you can meet people another way.

## When writing feels too cold

Some people come across better in the moment than on paper. The Live Crush menu is made for this: you search using your own criteria, invite people who are online right now, and the conversation opens for 1 Crush token. Someone who replies straight away because they're there changes the energy.

There are also Duo Nights, at set times. For shy people, they're precious: everyone is there for the same reason at the same moment, so nobody has to justify their first word.

Once the conversation is open in your messages, icebreakers help out when inspiration runs dry. They don't replace your voice, but they spare you the blank first line, and you can carry on in your own words afterwards.

## Tonight, just one detail

Open a profile. Look for one thing only: the sentence, photo or value that made you stop. Write about it in the first person, add a small, easy question, and reread it, asking yourself whether this message could be sent to anyone else. If it couldn't, send it, then put the phone down and go and eat. That's already a better start than two letters.
