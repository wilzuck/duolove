When she writes at 7 in the morning in Montreal, he's coming home from work in Cotonou, or the other way round, depending on the season. She has a coffee, he's hungry. She's thinking about her day as it begins, he's thinking about his as it ends. What passes between them isn't just sentences: it's two rhythms of life brushing against each other. Meeting someone when one of you lives in the diaspora and the other back home means learning that distance doesn't only change the journey. It changes the pace, the expectations, sometimes the meaning of words.

This piece isn't trying to discourage you or sell you a fairy tale. It brings together what's worth knowing before you get attached.

## Two clocks, one thread

The time difference is the first visitor, and it turns up unannounced. Between Paris or Brussels and West Africa, it's small, or even zero depending on the season. Between Montreal and Lomé, it weighs more. A conversation that flows nicely at noon for one of you lands in the middle of the night for the other.

A few simple habits change everything:

- **Set a rhythm rather than a constant stream.** A call on Sunday late morning, two messages on weekday evenings: that's more sustainable than being permanently available, which burns out in three weeks.
- **Say when you can't be reached.** "I'm in a meeting until 6 pm, talk after" stops silence being read as lack of interest.
- **Don't expect an instant reply.** A message you send at 11 pm your time may arrive in the middle of a busy day for the other person.
- **Share your surroundings.** "It's been raining here since this morning and the street smells of fresh bread" is better than "have a nice day". A bond is built with details, not superlatives.

Crushduo's messaging keeps the conversation in place: you pick up where you left off without having to recap everything. Calls are best kept for moments when you're both genuinely free: a rushed call squeezed between two obligations won't say what you want it to say.

## What each person expects (and doesn't always say)

Many long-distance misunderstandings come from expectations that never met. Three scenarios come up often, and nobody is in the wrong in any of them.

**Going back.** Some people in the diaspora think about the day they'll return home for good. They're looking for someone who shares the idea of a future back home, or who at least welcomes it.

**Marriage.** For others, the relationship has a clear horizon: building a family, with all the steps that go with it, including traditional ceremonies and introductions to parents. For others still, it isn't the question of the moment, and being pushed on it feels like pressure.

**A shared project.** A house, a business, a plot of land, a shop: the idea of building something concrete together is appealing, but it quickly mixes the heart with money. Nothing stops you talking about it. But don't let a project stand in for a relationship that doesn't exist yet.

The healthiest approach is to name your direction once trust starts to settle, without ultimatums: "I'm thinking of moving back home in a few years, what about you?" The answer shouldn't make you run. It informs you. The personality questions and values shown on profiles are a good starting point: they say something about a way of life before you go into detail.

## Money: the subject that changes weight with distance

This is where tact matters most. Someone in the diaspora often has cost-of-living reference points that aren't those of home, and vice versa. A coffee in Brussels, a taxi ride in Niamey, a phone bill in Montreal: the amounts don't carry the same weight, and nobody should feel judged for comparing them.

Misunderstandings most often come from a silent assumption. One thinks the other "can afford it", the other thinks the first "doesn't understand what it costs". Nobody lied, but two pictures were built separately.

A few guidelines to stay calm:

1. **Talk about your daily life, not your bank account.** Saying what you do, your outgoings, your family responsibilities, is enough to be understood without getting into figures.
2. **Don't let money become a language of affection.** Giving a gift is a gesture. Funding an emergency for someone you've never met is something else entirely.
3. **Decide your limit in advance.** Before anyone asks you for anything, know what you would be prepared to do and what you wouldn't. A limit set ahead of time is easier to hold than one improvised under pressure.

And one golden rule: as long as you've never spent time together, no money should pass between you for reasons of "trust". Trust is built over time; it isn't proved by a transfer.

## Video before the plane

A plane ticket is a heavy commitment: time, money, leave from work, sometimes the expectations of a whole family. Before you think about it, go through a cheaper and more revealing step: video.

A call with images, even just ten minutes, teaches you in one go what ten days of messages hide: the voice, the rhythm, the eyes, the way they listen, their unfiltered humour. It also confirms that the person really is the one in the photos, something the Verified profile badge and photo approval by moderation support without replacing.

Do it several times, at different times of day, in different places. Someone who happily agrees to a video call and shows you a bit of their everyday life (a kitchen, a courtyard, a street) is someone who wants to be seen. Someone who always finds a reason to avoid it is telling you something too, just in a different way.

A good threshold before talking about travel: having already shared several video calls, without pressure, where you laughed together and got through a small disagreement without drama.

## Don't idealise, in either direction

Distance is very good at manufacturing images. We fill the gaps with what we hope for. The person living "over there" becomes brighter, more stable, more available. The person living "here" becomes more open, more confident, wealthier. None of these images is true.

To keep them honest:

- Ask about ordinary days: what annoys you, what tires you out, the last time you lost your patience.
- Talk about your own days without embellishing. Showing your life as it is gives the other person permission to do the same.
- Be wary of perfection: an exchange where everything is always wonderful isn't a relationship, it's a shop window.
- Accept silences and dull days. A relationship that lasts can handle them.

And for those in the diaspora, one precaution not to overlook: the country you left has changed since. Your memories of the city, its customs, its prices, the way people court, date from another time. Listen to the person who lives there as the expert they are.

## Talk logistics and family early, gently

In many families, a serious relationship isn't a private matter. Parents, siblings and elders have their say, or at least their expectations. That's not an obstacle: it's part of the landscape. At a distance, it pays to bring it up before you're too attached, simply so nobody is caught off guard.

A few questions that open the conversation without weighing it down:

- What place does your family have in your decisions?
- What matters to them when you meet someone?
- At what point do people talk about introductions?
- Where could we meet, and how long can each of us be away?
- Who can travel, and under what conditions?

Talking about visas, time off, seasons and accommodation is less romantic, but it's what turns a plan to meet into something real. Again, none of this is legal advice: check with your country's official sources, and avoid promising what you don't control.

## The signals that should make you stop

The digital world also attracts people who are playing a role. Recognising a warning sign isn't paranoia, it's clear-sightedness. Watch out for:

- **A request for money, however small, however "temporary",** before you've met.
- **An emergency that always comes at just the right moment**: an accident, a medical bill, a parcel stuck in customs, a fine, a ticket that needs unblocking.
- **Pressure on time**: "if you don't help me today, it'll be too late".
- **Repeated refusal of video,** with reasons that keep changing.
- **A story that contradicts itself,** details of their life that shift from one conversation to the next.
- **Very fast declarations of love,** followed by a request for secrecy: "don't tell anyone".
- **A wish to leave the app** very early for a chat where you no longer have the same protections.

If any of these signals appears, slow down. Talk about it with someone you trust, someone who isn't under the spell. You can report and block the profile from the app without having to justify yourself. And if you need a breather, Pause mode gives you some distance without erasing what you've built.

## Keep a clear head without closing your heart

You can love carefully. You can even love better because you're careful. A few reflexes to keep at hand:

> What I feel is real. What I know about the other person is still partial.

Write down what you've been told and what you've seen with your own eyes: they're two different columns. Let both move forward together. Take the time not to reply straight away to a message that unsettles you. Move at the pace of reality, not the pace of hope.

For your next conversation with someone who lives far away: suggest a video call on a Sunday, talk about an ordinary day rather than a big plan, and slip in a question about family and the future, lightly, without expecting a final answer. Distance doesn't make love impossible. It just asks for a little more patience and a little more clarity.
