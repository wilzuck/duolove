On Crushduo, each profile you come across may show a compatibility percentage along with a few reasons: "you're looking for the same thing", "you live in the same city", "you share interests". A number is reassuring, but it can also mislead if you ask it to do something it can't.

This guide explains what your interests and values are for, how the percentage is built in broad terms, and above all how to use it as **a reading aid**, never as a verdict.

## What the percentage says, and what it doesn't

The percentage compares what you have filled in with what the other person has filled in: what you're looking for (a serious relationship, marriage, friendship…), how close you live, shared interests and certain preferences. It comes with readable explanations, so you understand where it comes from.

So it **does** say: "based on the information given, these two profiles are similar or complement each other on these points".

It **doesn't** say:

- whether the chemistry will be there: you can't measure that from a photo or a form;
- whether the other person is available, honest, or ready for a relationship;
- whether you'll agree on the important subjects nobody has had time to fill in on a profile.

A 78% isn't a promise, and a 45% isn't a sentence. It's a starting point for deciding **who is worth a first question**.

## Interests: choose them honestly

You can add interests to your profile: music, cooking, travel, faith, entrepreneurship, sport, reading, family, nature, video games… They serve two purposes: finding things in common and providing topics of conversation.

A few tips:

- **Choose what you actually do.** Ticking "travel" because it sounds good, when you've never left your city, creates a misunderstanding at the first exchange. Choose interests you could talk about for ten minutes without preparing.
- **Mix the popular with the specific.** Someone who ticks "music" is like everyone else. Add "photography" or "entrepreneurship" and they become more recognisable.
- **Don't tick everything.** A list that's too long dilutes the message and lowers trust: people wonder whether you really want to choose.
- **Update the list.** Your passions change. An update once a quarter keeps your profile from being frozen on the person you used to be.

## Life values: what really matters

Beyond hobbies, values say what matters to you: faith, family, ambition, honesty, a sense of service, stability, humour… In the long run, they often count for more than loving the same sport. Two people who adore football but don't share the same idea of family will clash sooner than two people with no hobbies in common who share the same priorities.

A few pointers for choosing them:

- **Think in priorities, not slogans.** If you had to pick three things that guide your decisions, what would they be?
- **Stay consistent with your real life.** A value you display but never practise shows from the very first dates.
- **Accept nuance.** You can care about both faith and freedom, family and independence. The values you display aren't boxes to tick to please people, but markers to attract people who are close to you.

## How to read a profile with the percentage in hand

When a profile is suggested to you, go step by step:

1. **Read the person first**, before the number: photos, bio, answers to the profile questions.
2. **Look at the reasons** given with the percentage: they matter far more than the number itself.
3. **Find a specific point of connection**: a rare interest, a sentence that made you smile, a shared value.
4. **Decide**: a Like, perhaps with a short note, or a pass with no regrets.

> The percentage is a signpost, not a GPS: it shows a direction, it doesn't choose the road.

## When a 40% profile deserves a chance

A modest score doesn't mean "avoid". It may simply reflect a profile that isn't filled in much: the person hasn't given a lot of information, so there are few things in common to measure. If their photos and bio touch you, a Like with a sincere message costs little. Likewise, a high score on a profile that doesn't attract you obliges you to nothing.

## And what about your own profile?

If you want relevant suggestions, fill in your information carefully. The more complete and honest your profile, the more the people you are shown resemble you, and the more the people you are shown to have a real reason to reply. A half-filled profile produces vague matches; a clear profile produces fewer encounters, but better ones.

## A short exercise to clarify your values

If you're unsure what really matters to you, try this. Take a sheet of paper and complete three sentences: "I couldn't live with someone who…", "I feel at my best when…", "In five years, I'd like my life to look like…". Then reread your answers: the words that come back are your real values. You'll often find that what seemed central (a hobby, a job) matters less than what sits in the background (respect, stability, freedom). It's that list, not the boxes you tick, that should guide your choices.

## Keep a cool head

The main risk with a number-based indicator is that you start dating percentages rather than people. A few habits help:

- **Don't sort by number.** Leave room for chance: an unexpected encounter is often worth more than an optimised one.
- **Don't look for 100%.** It doesn't exist. If your ideal is someone who ticks every box, you'll miss out on some wonderful people.
- **Trust your feelings.** If a conversation at 62% does you good and one at 91% bores you, listen to the first.

Interests and values aren't there to lock you into a category. They exist to bring together people who want to talk to each other. The rest, curiosity, attention, humour, patience, can't be measured, and that's just as well.
