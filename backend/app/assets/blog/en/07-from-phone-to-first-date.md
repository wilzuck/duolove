In every conversation that's going well, there comes a moment when the messages start going round in circles. You've told each other about your days, your families, the music you're into right now. You've laughed at the same joke three times. Then one evening, one of you writes "talk tomorrow?" when you've already said everything. That evening, the screen has done its job. It has brought you to the door; it won't walk through it for you.

This guide doesn't promise you the perfect date. It offers something better: a date prepared just enough for you to feel at ease, and not so much that everything is scripted in advance.

## The right moment isn't a date on the calendar, it's a signal

A lot of advice sets a number of days or messages. It's reassuring and it's wrong: some people write a lot because they enjoy it, others because they're bored, and others still because they're afraid of what comes next. The number of messages tells you nothing. Look for these signals instead:

- you no longer have "getting to know you" questions, just pure curiosity;
- you catch yourself imagining their voice, their laugh, the way they carry themselves;
- the conversation has already been through a small disagreement or an awkward moment, and stayed warm;
- you look forward to the next message with pleasure rather than a twinge of anxiety.

A simple test: if the person suggested coffee tomorrow, would your first reaction be a smile or a list of excuses? If it's a smile, you're ready. If it's a list of excuses, maybe something is missing: a call, a voice, something in between. Crushduo's messaging lets you make calls: fifteen minutes on the phone is often worth ten days of texting to find out whether the energy is there.

## Choosing the place: what a venue says before you even speak

A place speaks for you. A restaurant with white tablecloths signals a serious evening; a table under a mango tree, a low-stakes afternoon. For a first date, look for somewhere that meets four conditions: it's public, you can leave easily, you can talk without shouting, and it doesn't cost much for whoever arrives first.

A few ideas that work well, depending on where you live:

- **A shady terrace, during the day.** You're seen without being scrutinised. The waiter brings welcome interruptions when a silence stretches.
- **A quiet maquis, a local open-air eatery, before the rush.** If you go at 4 pm rather than 9 pm, you get the atmosphere without the volume. Order drinks, don't commit to a meal.
- **A garden, a park, a café courtyard.** Your hands have room, and so do your eyes: you can look at something other than the person opposite, which is a relief.
- **A café whose menu you know.** You already know what you'll order, so your mind is free for the conversation.
- **A walk by the lagoon or the sea, during the day.** Walking side by side removes the face-to-face pressure and makes silences feel natural. It's the format that's kindest to shyness.

Avoid places where people know you too well (your neighbour's aunt is a regular) and places where you need to book weeks ahead. Plan for something short: an hour or two. A date that ends while you'd like to keep going is a good date.

Tell someone you trust where you're going and at what time. It isn't distrust, it's basic hygiene, like putting on your seatbelt.

## Who pays? Settle it before it comes up

The awkwardness almost always comes from what goes unsaid. Two people look at the bill, each waiting for the other to make a move, and three minutes of warmth evaporate. You can avoid this in several ways, all of them perfectly honourable.

**Set the terms when you invite.** "Let me treat you to a juice, does that work for you?" settles everything. So does: "We each pay our share, OK?" What matters is being clear.

**Pick a budget that worries nobody.** If the place is modest, the question of the bill becomes almost trivial. Two drinks and a small snack is something anyone can pay for without doing the maths.

**Plan to take turns.** "Today's on me, next time you choose" hints at a next time without forcing it. If the other person declines your offer to pay, that's their right: don't turn a refusal into offence.

And one rule for everyone: whoever invites isn't buying anything. Paying gives no rights and creates no debt. If your date makes you feel otherwise, you've learned something useful.

## Suggesting a time and place without it sounding like a summons

The hardest part isn't wanting to meet, it's saying so without scaring yourself. A phrasing that eases things: offer an option rather than an order. "Saturday afternoon, a coffee near the market, would you be up for it? If not, tell me what suits you." The other person can accept, adjust or decline without it turning into a scene.

That's exactly what proposing a date on Crushduo is for: in the conversation, you suggest a time and a place, and the other person responds to that same proposal. No messages lost between two jokes, no "what did we say again?". The proposal stays visible, clear and editable. If the place doesn't suit, you just say so and suggest another.

One detail that matters: suggest a specific time, not "one of these days". Vague dates die of polite evasions.

## The first thirty minutes

Almost everything happens at the start, not because it's an exam, but because that's when nerves speak loudest. A few concrete ideas.

**Arrive ten minutes early, no more.** Enough time to choose your seat, breathe and look around. If you're running late, send a short message rather than a long apology.

**Have three topics in reserve,** not a programme. Something you've read, a place you love, a question about their work or what they're passionate about. You may not use any of them, but knowing they're there is calming.

**Start with what's in front of you.** The place, the drink, the light, the journey there. The best conversations grow out of an observation, not a survey question.

**Put your phone away, really.** Face down on the table, it's still a third person at the table. In your pocket, it disappears. If you're expecting an important call, say so at the start: "I might have to answer, it's a family matter."

**Accept awkwardness.** A silence, a laugh at the wrong moment, a clumsy sentence: these are signs that you're two human beings, not two profiles. What you wrote so carefully on the screen, you'll only roughly manage to say. In real life, we stumble a bit, and that's fine.

After thirty minutes, you'll already know something: not whether this is "the one", but whether you want to stay another fifteen minutes. That's the only useful question.

## Saying "I don't feel the spark" gracefully

It happens, even after lovely messages. Someone can be charming, funny and attentive, and still not give you that little rush that makes you say "yes, more". Nobody is to blame.

What hurts isn't the no, it's the silence that replaces it. Ghosting someone who came to meet you in person is the only real rudeness in this game. Here's a simple way to say it:

> "I had a really good time with you, and thank you for being so open. I'd rather be honest: I don't feel the spark I'm looking for. I genuinely wish you some great encounters."

It takes four sentences and requires no justification or detail. You can say it at the end of the date, with a smile, or in a message the next day. Don't say "let's talk soon" if you don't mean it. And if the other person is the one saying it: thank them, don't argue, don't look for the argument that would change their mind. A "no" received with grace is the finest impression you can leave.

If, on the other hand, a date was unpleasant, or the other person crossed a line, you owe them no elegance: you can block and report them from the app. Your safety comes before politeness.

## A good date isn't necessarily a love story

We tend to judge a first date by what it leads to: a second date, a kiss, a relationship. Change the measure. A good date is an hour where you were yourself, where you listened, where you went home with something more than you came with: an idea, a laugh, a desire, or simply certainty about what you don't want.

Before you leave, keep one simple question in mind: "Did I feel like talking, or like defending myself?" The answer tells you more than any list of qualities.

For your next invitation: choose a relaxed place, say who's paying, keep a few questions in reserve, and leave half the evening to the unexpected. It's in that half that, sometimes, the surprise slips in.
