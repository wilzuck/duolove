Some conversations get put off because we're afraid of seeming heavy. The one where you ask someone whether they want children, what place prayer has in their week, what they think about the money you send back to the village. You tell yourself: "I'll see later." And later, you're already attached, and the question cuts like a knife.

Talking about your values doesn't have to sound like a sermon. It can be a sentence slipped in between two jokes, on a motorbike-taxi ride, or while you're peeling onions on a call, one earbud in. Here's how to go about it.

## What do we actually mean by "values"?

The word sounds solemn. In practice, a value is what decides for you when you don't have time to think. When your sister calls at midnight about an urgent expense, when Sunday comes and you hesitate between church, the mosque or a lie-in, when you're asked to host the whole family for a celebration even though you'd planned something else.

Those reflexes sketch a character far better than a list of qualities. Someone who writes "I'm honest and funny" tells you almost nothing. Someone who writes "I call my mother every Sunday after lunch, even when I'm exhausted" shows you a life.

## Preference or non-negotiable: the sorting that prevents misunderstandings

Many disagreements come from a mix-up. We treat a mere taste as a barrier, or the reverse: we smile and let something slide that, six months later, will shake the whole house.

A simple way to sort: picture the situation as it would be lived, not the abstract sentence.

- **A preference** is something you'd like, but could live without after some negotiation. You'd prefer someone who cooks a bit? You'll survive a partner who'd rather order in.
- **A non-negotiable** is something whose absence or presence would make you betray yourself every day. You want to become a parent and the other person is sure they never will: no amount of goodwill will close that gap.

A useful test: "If I get everything else but not this, am I happy?" If the answer is no, you've found a non-negotiable. If the answer is "it would bother me, but yes", it's a preference, and it's best mentioned lightly.

Keep your list of non-negotiables short. When everything is sacred, nothing is, and the other person can't find a way in.

## The five sensitive areas, one by one

### Faith

Your practice might be private, regular, communal, or non-existent, and that's exactly the question: how does it fill your week? Talking about your faith isn't about convincing. It's about describing. "I go to church on Sunday mornings, it's my quiet time" is information. "Everyone should come with me" is an invitation to get defensive.

The real thing to clarify is often practical: how children will be raised, celebrations, meals, what you expect from a spouse who doesn't share the same practice. Ask about its place, not about doctrine.

### Family and in-laws

In many families, you never marry just one person. The role of the in-laws plays out in the details: who gets consulted before a decision, who stays with whom, who receives the surprise visits. Say what you expect, and what you offer. "My family means a great deal to me, and I like us to see each other often" is clearer with an addition: "but I need our decisions as a couple to be made by the two of us."

### Children

Yes or no to parenthood, a rough timeline, the number you dream of, children who are already there. This subject needs to come up before attachment, early but without bluntness. A line like "I see myself with children one day, what about you?" is enough to open it.

### Life plans

Living here or elsewhere, going back home one day or building a life on the other side, supporting your parents from afar. For the diaspora especially, geography is part of your values: where is "home"? Life plans are best discussed like a route, not a verdict.

### Money

It's not the amount that reveals someone, it's their relationship with it. Saving or enjoying, helping the extended family or not, sharing everything or keeping separate accounts. Talking about money is really talking about security and loyalty. Avoid figures at first; talk about principles.

## Putting them on your profile without it sounding like a rulebook

The classic trap: the list of demands. "I want someone serious, a believer, no children, no past." It reads like a job specification, and nobody wants to apply for a job specification.

Choose the scene over the adjective. On Crushduo, your profile can carry your values and your answers to profile questions: use that space to show, not to decree.

- Instead of "I want a pious woman", try: "Morning prayer is my anchor; I'm looking for someone who respects that rhythm, whether or not it's the same as theirs."
- Instead of "no interfering in-laws", try: "I love big family tables. I also like decisions made by two."
- Instead of "no easy money", try: "I keep track of what I spend and prefer to build slowly."

One true sentence is worth ten adjectives. It also filters without aggression: those it frightens move on, those it touches write to you. A verified badge and photos approved by moderation already reassure people about your identity; your words give the direction.

## In conversation: the right moment, the right dose

There's a quiet timeline. The first exchanges are for seeing whether you like each other. Once the conversation has settled in, slip a values question into an ordinary exchange, never in a barrage. The icebreakers in the chat can be a light way in; a short note with a Like can already hint at what matters to you.

A few ways to open up without interrogating:

1. **Tell your own story first.** "This Sunday everyone was at my aunt's, fifteen people, one cooking pot. What's it like at yours?"
2. **Ask an open question.** "What makes you feel at peace in a busy week?"
3. **React to an answer on their profile.** It's a natural foothold: they've already chosen to say it.

Avoid the run of questions and answers that turns into a job interview. A value shows itself better in an anecdote than in a questionnaire: the way someone describes their last trip back to the village says more than their answer to "what do you think about family?"

## When the other person doesn't think like you

Disagreement isn't failure. It's information that arrived early, which is lucky. There are three honest outcomes.

- **The gap is small and feels light.** You carry on, curious.
- **The gap is real but negotiable.** You come back to it later, when trust is stronger.
- **The gap touches a non-negotiable.** You say so gently and don't try to convince.

To take a disagreement in without raising your voice, one sentence helps: "I understand this matters to you, and for me it matters differently." Don't give in, don't attack. If the conversation ends there, it can end cleanly, without blame; another article on this blog is about the art of ending a conversation without hurting anyone.

And if you're the one whose values are turned down, don't translate that into a verdict on your worth as a person. Someone who says "this isn't compatible" isn't saying "you're not enough".

## A checklist to fill in alone, before you swipe

Take ten minutes, a pen and a quiet moment, perhaps after dinner. Answer without worrying about what your loved ones would say.

1. Among my topics, which are non-negotiables, and how many are there? If I have more than four or five, which ones truly are?
2. If I had to describe my ideal Sunday, what would you see?
3. What place do I want my family, and the other person's, to have in our decisions?
4. Do I want children, roughly when, and what do I do if the other person says no?
5. What's my relationship with money when all is well, and when someone close asks for help?
6. Where do I see myself living in a few years?
7. On which point am I genuinely ready to move?
8. What simple, true sentence could I write on my profile about one of these points?

The answers aren't for display. They help you know what you're looking for, so that your choice of profiles follows a compass rather than a mood.

## What to take with you

A value shared early and gently prevents more pain than it causes. Sort your non-negotiables, write them as scenes, ask your questions as invitations. If someone answers differently from you, it's precious to know before the heart commits. And the next time you open the app, you'll know what you came looking for.
