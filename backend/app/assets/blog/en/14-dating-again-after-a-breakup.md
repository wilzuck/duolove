There comes a day when you catch yourself opening your phone again without quite knowing why. Perhaps after a wedding where you danced a little too long, or because a friend said, casually: "You know, you could." On that day, nothing has been decided. There's just a little curiosity, and beside it, a great tiredness.

This piece is for anyone who has been through a breakup, a divorce or the loss of a partner, and is wondering whether, how and at what pace to start meeting someone again. There's no right speed. There's yours.

## "Am I ready?": a question with no clear answer

We'd like a clear signal, a bell ringing to say: now. There isn't one. You're rarely ready all at once. You're a little ready on Tuesday, much less so on Sunday evening, and a little again the following Thursday.

A few clues, rather than certainties:

- You can talk about the person you lost or left without your voice breaking at every sentence, even if it still wobbles sometimes.
- You want to share something about your day with someone, not just fill a void.
- The idea of meeting someone new intrigues you more than it frightens you.
- You're not looking for a replacement, or for proof that you're still worth something.

If several of these ring true, you can give it a try. If none of them resonates, that's not a failure: it means the time for home, friends and silence isn't over yet. And if you try too soon, nothing is lost either. You can always put the phone aside.

## Your story isn't a case file

Many people coming back to dating have the same reflex: explain everything, straight away, to "be honest". The divorce, the reasons, who was at fault, the children, the grief. They present their past like a document to be signed before anyone can come in.

Yet you can be completely sincere without pouring everything out in the first message. Your story belongs to you. It's told in pieces, as trust grows. "I was married, I'm not any more" is information. What happened in the kitchen one evening in March three years ago is a story that will come, or not, when you decide.

On a profile, one simple, clear sentence works better than a long paragraph explaining everything: "Divorced, mum to a little girl, I love markets and slow conversations." That's enough for the other person to know where they stand, without you having to justify yourself.

## Talking about your children, without making it a test

If you have children, they're part of your life, and it's normal not to hide them. But there's a difference between mentioning them and making them the only subject.

Say it early, without embarrassment, the way you'd mention your job or your city: "I have two boys, they're my priority." Then let the conversation breathe. Avoid framing it as a trap: "Kids don't bother you, right?" In reality, nobody can answer that honestly in a first exchange. Ask instead: "What would you like to know about my life?"

As for the children themselves, there's no hurry. You don't have to introduce anyone until you yourself are sure, and until that person has shown, over time, that they know how to be there. Protecting your children from a parade of faces isn't coldness, it's tenderness.

If it's the other person who has children, welcome them as part of their world, not as competition. Ask questions, be curious, and don't rush into a role you haven't been given yet.

## Going at your own pace, even if the world moves faster

People around you will have opinions. "You need to get back in the saddle." "Wait a bit longer, you're too fragile." "At your age, you can't afford to hang about." Each of them speaks from their own fear or their own experience.

Your pace can be very slow: reading profiles for weeks without writing to anyone. Or very fast: three conversations in an evening and a date on the horizon. Both are legitimate as long as they do you good.

On Crushduo, this can be quite concrete. You can browse your Daily picks without sending anything. You can answer the Question of the day just for the pleasure of putting a thought into words. You can also switch on Pause mode when it feels like too much, and come back a month later. If you'd rather stay in the background at first, Incognito mode hides your activity status, depending on your plan. None of this obliges you to make progress.

## Don't compare, or forgive yourself for comparing

It's almost inevitable: the first person you like, you'll measure against the one before. They laugh more quietly, or more loudly. They make coffee differently. They don't say your name the way the other one did.

The trap is believing these comparisons are betrayals or verdicts. They're simply the trace of a long habit. You'll never compare two people fairly, because one was lived alongside for years and the other for three conversations.

When it happens, you can tell yourself gently: "Ah, I'm thinking of them." Then come back to what's in front of you, today. For widowhood in particular, there's no competition between the memory of the person who has gone and the person who is arriving. The love you had isn't replaced; it's carried, and it can live alongside a new affection, with its own colours.

## Accepting that you'll feel awkward

After many years as part of a couple, flirting has changed its face. The codes aren't the same, and you feel you've forgotten how to write a first message, how to crack a joke, how to stay natural with someone you don't know.

That awkwardness isn't a flaw. It's even touching, and often shared: on the other side of the screen, the other person may be just as hesitant as you. Give yourself the right to slightly wobbly sentences, to silences, to "I'm not quite sure how to put this".

A few simple supports can help:

1. Use the icebreakers in the chat when you don't know where to start, then drift towards your own way of talking.
2. Send a Like with a short, sincere note rather than a one-size-fits-all compliment.
3. Describe your state of mind without dramatising: "I'm easing back in, taking my time, and I'm enjoying chatting with you."
4. Prefer a short, warm exchange to a long letter you'll be nervous about the next day.

The people worth meeting understand this kind of frankness very well.

## The role of friends: neither judge nor conductor

Friends are often your best support, as long as you choose the right ones for the job. A friend who listens without correcting, who accepts that you've changed your mind three times in a week, is worth their weight in gold.

You can ask them for specific things. That one of them knows where you're going and at what time when you have a date, and that you send them a quick message afterwards. That another reads over your profile text. That a third simply walks you to a café before letting you go in alone, for the pleasure of having someone to tell about it afterwards.

Say what you don't need, too: comparisons with their own story, unsolicited advice, pressure about the timeline. A close friend will understand, and if not, you can talk about it with someone else.

## When the sadness stays

Sometimes the pain lingers longer than you expected, and even the good days have a grey background. If this goes on and weighs on you, talk to someone you trust, or to a professional: it's a way of taking care of yourself, not an admission of weakness.

## Looking ahead

Dating again doesn't mean turning the page or erasing what has been. It means opening a door, at the pace you want, and closing it when you need to. You have nothing to prove, to yourself or to anyone. A half-written message, a pleasant conversation, an accepted coffee: each of these small gestures already counts, and you have all the time you need for the next ones.
