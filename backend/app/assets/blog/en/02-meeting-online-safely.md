Picture a kitchen on a Saturday morning. Your cousin is peeling yams, you're perched on a stool with your phone in your hand, and you show her a profile. She doesn't look at the photo first. She asks three questions: "What does he do? How does he talk? Have you ever seen him smile for real?" Without realising it, she has just done what careful people do: look for consistency.

Meeting people online is nothing to be scared of if you keep that habit. It's not about suspecting everyone, but about having a few calm reference points, the way you look both ways before crossing the road.

## Trust is built through small proofs

We don't trust all at once, whether it's a neighbour, a new colleague or someone met on an app. We give a little, we watch, we give a little more. That progression is healthy, and it's even a form of courtesy: it gives the other person time to show who they are.

A good habit is to decide in advance what you're ready to do at each stage. Today, exchanging messages. In a few days, a call. Later, a coffee in broad daylight. At each step, you check that what you feel matches what you see.

## Checking that someone is real

On Crushduo, profile photos are approved by moderation. Some profiles also carry the Verified profile badge. These are real markers and they count: a verified profile has gone through an extra step. But no badge replaces your own attention, and that isn't its job. Here's what you can look at yourself.

- **Does the profile say something specific?** Answers to the personality questions, values, details of daily life. A real profile has rough edges, preferences, sometimes contradictions. An empty or overly polished profile deserves a closer look.
- **Do the photos look consistent with each other?** Same person, same look, different settings, different moments. If one photo seems to come from a magazine and the next from another world entirely, ask yourself why.
- **Do their answers match the profile?** If someone wrote that they love their grandmother's cooking but, in conversation, can't say anything about it, or keeps changing the story, that's worth noting.
- **Can they answer a simple question?** Ask something very concrete: "Which neighbourhood do you have your morning coffee in?" Real people tell you. Others dodge or answer something else.

None of these signs proves anything on its own. It's the overall picture that speaks, like judging a fabric: you touch it, look at it, compare.

## What you should never send

This is the most important part, and it only takes a few lines. Whoever the person is, whatever the situation, however strong your feelings in the moment, you don't send:

1. **Money.** Not for an emergency, not for a ticket, not for a hospital bill, not to "help out until tomorrow". Someone who really likes you can wait until you've actually met before talking about their difficulties.
2. **Codes.** A code received by text, a confirmation code, the code for your mobile money account: they're yours alone. Nobody needs to know them to love you.
3. **Documents.** ID, travel papers, family documents. A relationship that is just beginning doesn't need them.

These aren't rules of distrust, they're house rules: just as you wouldn't hand your keys to someone you met the day before, however kind they seem. If the conversation turns tense as soon as you say no, that's the most useful information you'll get all week.

Having a ready-made sentence helps, because words can fail you under pressure: "I never do that, it's nothing personal." Short, calm, final.

## Report, block, take a break

If something makes you uncomfortable, you have tools, and you don't need to justify using them. Reporting lets you alert the team about a profile or message that seems out of line. Blocking ends contact: the person can no longer write to you. You can do both, in either order.

Sometimes the problem isn't a person but your energy: too many messages, too many decisions, a busy week. Pause mode is made for that. You put things on hold, breathe, and come back when you decide to. Stepping away for a while isn't giving up.

## Incognito mode: moving forward without exposing yourself

Not everyone wants to be seen by everybody from day one. That's true for a private person, for someone whose family or workplace is in the same city, or simply for anyone who wants to watch before diving in. Incognito mode, included in some plans, is designed for this: it hides your activity status (online, last seen), so others can't tell when you're there. It doesn't hide your profile, but it lets you explore more discreetly. It's a perfectly normal choice, and there's no right way to start.

## The first date: a public place, in daylight

When the time comes, once the conversation has become enjoyable and you've had time to check that what the person says stays consistent, you can move on to meeting. Proposing a date in the conversation is there to support that step from the screen to the table. A few choices are enough to make the moment feel relaxed.

- **A busy place.** A café, a restaurant you know, a terrace with people passing by. Somewhere you feel at ease, that you chose or approved yourself.
- **A daytime slot.** Late afternoon rather than late at night. You think more clearly, getting there is easier, and getting home doesn't turn into a headache.
- **Your own transport.** You arrive on your own and you leave on your own. Moto-taxi, taxi, bus, car: it doesn't matter, but you stay in control of when you leave.
- **Keep it short.** An hour, a juice or a coffee. If there's chemistry, there will be a second date. There's no rush.
- **Stay sober.** Keep a clear head, especially the first time. You can raise a glass later.

For people in the diaspora, the same logic applies in an adapted form: take time for video calls before travelling, choose a public place in a city you know, and don't plan a trip around a relationship that is still young.

## Tell someone close to you, simply

Tell someone where you're going, with whom, at what time, and when you'll check in. It isn't suspicion, it's what you'd do for any slightly new outing. A friend, a brother, a colleague you trust: a two-line message is enough.

> "I'm meeting someone at 4 pm at the café near the market. I'll text you at 6 pm to say everything's fine."

Then keep your word and text at the time you said. If you'd rather not explain the story to your family, choose the person you feel comfortable with: what matters is that someone knows.

And if, on the way, your instinct tells you something is wrong, listen to it. You have the right to leave, to change your mind, to simply say: "Actually, I'm going to head home." No explanation needed.

## A checklist to keep in your pocket

Before each new meeting, reread these points: the profile is consistent, the conversation has taken its time, no money, code or document has been asked for, the place is public, someone close to you knows. If one of them is missing, it's not a disaster: it's a signal to wait a little. Good stories don't run away because you take the time to check them.
