Your phone buzzes while you are peeling onions. A match. You wipe your hands on your wrap, look at the person's profile, and your heart does that slightly silly little jump nobody will see. Then comes the real question, the one almost nobody dares say out loud: what do I do now?

A match is not a relationship. It is a door left ajar, and two people who each thought, on their own side: "Why not?" What happens in the next two days decides nothing, but it often sets the tone. Here is how to approach that window calmly, with a little method, and without playing a role.

## What is really at stake in those two days

During the first forty-eight hours, nobody knows each other yet. So you are not judging the person: you are judging the impression they leave. An impression is fragile. It is built from almost nothing: how quickly a message comes, how long it is, the feeling of being read or of being treated as a number.

There is no magic rule here. But there is a shared wish that people often forget: both of you want this to go well. The person waiting for your message is probably just as nervous as you are. Keep that in mind, it softens everything.

## The first message: a bridge, not an exam

If you sent a Like with a short note, the conversation already starts with a sentence from you. If the other person reacted to your photo, one of your answers or one of your interests, they have already told you what caught their attention. Either way, you are not writing into a void.

For the first exchange, a good message does three things: it picks up a real detail, it fits in two or three sentences, and it leaves a door open without demanding a life story.

Take Aminata, in Abidjan, who has just matched with Koffi. She does not write "Hi, how are you?". She writes: "You put football and cooking in your interests. Are you more the type to watch the match with something simmering, or to forget the pot because of the match?" A warm opener, a detail, a little question that can be answered in one line. Koffi does not have to make an effort to reply, and he can tell he has been read.

If the block is "I don't know where to start", the messaging offers icebreakers. That is not cheating: it is a way to put down a first word when your head is empty, and you can personalise it afterwards with a detail of your own.

## Reply rhythm: follow along, don't keep time

This is where many beginnings break. You hear everywhere that you should "play hard to get", wait three hours before replying, never write twice in a row. That advice turns a meeting into a chess game, when all you want is someone to laugh with.

A simpler principle: reply when you are available, at the pace of your real life. If you are squeezed into a packed minibus, you don't reply. When you are sitting down, you reply with care. That honest rhythm is easy to read, and it reassures: the other person sees someone who has a life and still takes a moment to write to them.

A few useful pointers:

- Roughly match the other person's length. Two lines received, two or three lines back. A paragraph received calls for a real paragraph.
- Don't leave a message hanging when you have time to answer it. A simple "I'm on my way, I'll reply tonight" is better than nothing.
- If you know you will be unreachable, say so once, simply. "I'm travelling tomorrow, I'll read you in the evening." That saves two days of guesswork.

## The three mistakes that kill a beginning

### The barrage

Seven messages in a row, a selfie, an "are you there?", a second "are you there?". Even with good intentions, a barrage says: "I'm already very invested and I'm watching for your reaction." It puts on pressure the other person did not ask for. Write one complete message rather than five fragments. Then let it breathe.

### The interrogation

"What do you do for a living? Where do you live? Do you have children? When do you want to get married?" Each question is fair on its own. Fired off in a row, they turn the conversation into a form. A simple rule: for every question you ask, offer something about yourself. You give, you ask, you give. The exchange becomes a swap rather than a check-up.

### The calculated silence

Disappearing for two days "to see what happens" is the most common trap. Often the other person does not understand the strategy, they understand a lack of interest. And they move on, which is perfectly normal. If you want to reply, reply. If you don't, say that too, kindly, rather than leaving a ghost behind.

## Showing interest without overdoing it

People often confuse interest with intensity. Yet interest shows in quiet gestures:

1. **Remember a detail.** "You told me your sister is getting married in December, how are the preparations going?" Nothing flatters more than being listened to.
2. **React rather than just reply.** The messaging lets you add reactions and reply to a specific message. When the other person writes four things at once, answering the one that touched you shows you read to the end.
3. **Laugh wholeheartedly.** A light sentence, a nod to their joke, counts for more than a compliment on their looks.
4. **Share a small piece of your day.** Not a novel: a detail. The fabric pattern you couldn't decide on, the uncle who told the same story for the twelfth time.
5. **Use the Question of the day.** If the app offers you one, it is the perfect excuse: "I came across the Question of the day, what would you answer?" You are not asking for a confession, you are starting a game.

## Give yourself time to get to know them

There is a silent pressure after a match: to go fast, to know straight away whether "it clicks". Resist it. Two days are not enough to judge someone, and that is a good thing. They only help you answer one question: "Do I want to carry on?"

A few ways to keep a clear head. Notice how you feel after each exchange: lighter, or more tired? That is worth more than a list of qualities. Don't draw conclusions from a single bad message: some people write badly and talk well, or the other way round. And don't build a whole scenario from three sentences. The name of your future child can wait.

If you want to understand the person a little better without asking ten questions, reread their profile between messages. Their profile questions and their values often hold threads to pull on, which feeds the conversation without making it feel like an interrogation.

## When to suggest a longer exchange

After a day or two, a message sometimes feels too short for everything you want to say. That is usually the right signal to suggest something else, but not too early.

Signs the ground is ready: replies are getting longer, you catch yourself smiling at the screen, questions come back from both sides, and the rhythm has become natural. You can then suggest a voice call, simply: "I prefer talking when it gets long to type, would you fancy a quick voice call one evening this week?" Voice and video calls are available between two people in an active duo, and a voice or a face clears up a lot of misunderstandings.

Suggest it as an option, never as a test. If the other person says "not just yet", that is not a rejection: sometimes it is a need for time, sometimes a question of comfort. Accept that pace, you have lost nothing. And if what you feel like is talking live with people who are available right now, the Live Crush menu lets you invite people who are online: it is another door, not an obligation.

## What if the other person replies slowly?

This is the part that hurts the ego, so let's look at it closely. A slow reply almost never means what your imagination makes it mean. The person is working, looking after children, sharing a phone, has no signal in their area, or simply doesn't react quickly to messages. Add busy weeks, ceremonies and family trips: someone else's schedule is not a verdict on your worth.

Here is a calm way to handle it:

- **Write one complete message, then get on with your day.** Don't watch the screen.
- **Don't follow up too soon.** One light word after a day or two is enough: "I've been thinking about your question from the other night, did you find the answer?" No reproach, no "you're ignoring me".
- **Look at the pattern, not one isolated case.** Someone slow but present, who replies with care, is very different from someone who replies curtly and rarely.
- **Keep other conversations open.** That betrays nobody: it stops all your hopes resting on one pair of shoulders.

If nothing comes after a few days, you can let the conversation rest, or close it kindly. And if something about the other person's behaviour bothers you, reporting and blocking are there to protect you, with no need to justify yourself.

## An example, from the match to Thursday evening

On Tuesday, Awa matches with Serge. She writes to him that same evening, two sentences about his love of gardening, which she saw in his interests. He replies the next day at lunchtime, with a photo of his tomatoes. She laughs, reacts to the photo, then adds a little story about her aunt's basil. By Wednesday evening the conversation has some depth: they are talking about their favourite markets. On Thursday, Serge suggests a short voice call on Sunday, after lunch. Awa says yes.

Nothing extraordinary in this story. No tricks, no strategy. Just two people who replied when they were available, who listened to each other, and who let what came next arrive at the right time.

A match opens a door, it does not ask you to run. Write with a real detail, reply at your real pace, give as much as you ask, and suggest a voice when writing starts to feel too narrow. If the other person is slow, give them some air. And whatever happens in those forty-eight hours, remember you only need to know one thing: do you want to keep talking to this person?
