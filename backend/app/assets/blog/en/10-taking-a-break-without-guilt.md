One Thursday evening, Awa realises she has opened the app four times since dinner. Not to write to anyone. Just to look. She already knows there's nothing new, she's only just checked, but her thumb goes back on its own, the way you reopen an empty fridge.

If you recognise the scene, this piece is for you. It isn't about "giving up" or "doing a detox". It's about something more modest and more useful: learning to put the app down without making a big deal of it.

## When you feel the weight of it

Online dating fatigue doesn't look like big, dramatic tiredness. It's quiet. You sigh when you see the icon. Your replies are shorter than they used to be. You compare: this profile is funnier, that person has more photos, someone else replies faster. You catch yourself rereading a message you sent an hour ago to check it wasn't clumsy.

None of these signs is serious. They simply say your attention is saturated. Every profile is a small decision, every conversation a small wait, and small waits, added together, take up a lot of room in a day.

Here are a few signs that a break would do you good:

- you open the app out of reflex, without any particular desire;
- a missing reply ruins your evening;
- you compare more than you discover;
- you no longer enjoy reading people's introductions, you just feel like you're sorting.

Just one of these is enough. No need to wait until you're at the end of your tether.

## Waiting for a reply, the uninvited lodger

Let's talk about that one, because it's universal. You write a message, you put the phone down, and part of your mind stays hooked on the conversation. It checks, it imagines, it calculates: "they've read it, so…", "they were online, and yet…".

Waiting behaves like a lodger who doesn't pay rent: it takes up space in your home without being invited. The good news is that you can give it visiting hours.

Try this: decide to check your conversations at two moments in the day, for example late morning and after dinner. The rest of the time, your phone is just a phone. At first it feels odd. After a few days, the reply you were waiting for arrives, or it doesn't, but your day has got its substance back.

And if someone doesn't reply, remember that a silence has a thousand ordinary reasons: a flat battery, a busy week, a phone left at a cousin's. It isn't a judgement on you.

## Set times, cut the noise

A break doesn't have to be total to work. You can take it in small doses.

### A time slot, not a running tap

Choose a window when the app is allowed to exist: thirty minutes in the early evening, sitting down, a cup of tea beside you. When the slot ends, you close it. You'll find you read profiles better when you're focused, and write livelier messages when you're not in a rush.

### Notifications first

They're the ones tugging at your sleeve. On Crushduo, you can set **quiet hours**: you choose periods when notifications won't disturb you, at night, at work, during Sunday lunch. There's also a **daily limit on emails**, so your inbox doesn't fill up with every event. These are small settings that, together, bring back some silence.

### Incognito mode, to feel less exposed

If what weighs on you is the feeling of being watched, **Incognito mode**, included in some plans, can lighten things: it hides your activity status, so nobody sees when you're online. Your profile stays visible, but you no longer feel observed.

## Pause mode, when you really need to breathe

Sometimes you need more than set times. That's what **Pause mode** is for. While your account is paused, your profile is no longer put forward, you're no longer being solicited, and **notifications are muted**. Nothing is deleted: your photos, conversations and settings stay where they are, ready for when you come back.

There's a psychological benefit too, in the simplest sense: knowing you're officially on a break silences the little voice saying "you ought to reply". You no longer have to justify yourself to yourself.

And you don't have to announce anything. No need to write to everyone to explain. If one conversation really matters to you, a short note is enough: "I'm taking a few days for myself, I'll get back to you." It's honest, it's short, and most people understand it perfectly well.

## Coming back gently

The return is a delicate moment, because you tend to want to catch up on everything. Resist that.

1. **Don't start with your inbox.** Look at your own profile first: a photo to change, a sentence to shorten?
2. **Start again with a short slot.** A little while, not a whole evening.
3. **Reply to just one conversation** that you feel like continuing, not all the ones that have piled up.
4. **Keep your quiet hours switched on.** The break is over, not the need for calm.

If, on returning, you feel it isn't the right time, you can pause again. There's no entrance exam and no wrong way to come back.

## When you've been hurt

Sometimes the break comes not from tiredness but from words that stung: a curt message, a conversation that stopped dead, someone who wasn't who they seemed to be. You feel foolish, when there's no reason to.

A few simple steps. If someone behaved badly, you can **block** and **report** them: it protects others too, and it saves you from rereading an exchange that turns your stomach. That isn't holding a grudge, it's hygiene.

Then let time pass without forcing yourself to "have moved on". Talk about it with someone who knows you, or write it down on paper you keep to yourself. There's no correct length of time to recover, and nobody can dictate it to you. If the sadness becomes heavy and lasting, talking to a professional is a good idea, but this isn't the place where those answers lie.

## Turning towards the living

The loveliest paradox of a break is that it brings you back to people you'd slightly forgotten. While waiting filled your head, your friends were still there.

Here are a few concrete things to do with the time you free up:

- call a friend for no reason, just to hear their voice;
- accept the invitation you've been putting off for a while;
- go back to something you used to love: cooking a dish that takes time, running, drawing, a neighbourhood football game, fixing something;
- go for a walk without earphones, and look at what's around you.

This isn't about setting real life against the app as if you had to pick a side. A full life simply makes dating lighter: you come to a first message with something to say, and without the feeling that everything hangs on the reply.

## A break isn't a disappearance

Let's finish with what causes the most guilt: the feeling of letting people down. You owe nothing to an algorithm, and very little to a three-day-old conversation. Looking after your energy is what allows you to be truly present on the day a meeting matters.

If tonight you've opened the app out of reflex for the fifth time, try this: switch on quiet hours, put your phone in another room, and do something else until tomorrow. The profiles will still be there, and so will you, a little more available.
