At first, everything flows. Messages follow one another, you are discovering the other person, you can't wait to read the next reply. Then, often after a few days, the pace slows. Replies get shorter, questions become rare, and you catch yourself searching for something to write.

Don't worry: this is almost inevitable. A written conversation runs on a burst of energy that always fades eventually. The real question isn't how to avoid the lull, but what to do with it: revive it cleverly, turn the exchange into something else, or accept that the story ends there.

## Signs that a conversation is running out of steam

Before acting, you need to recognise the situation. The most common signs:

- **Replies that get shorter and shorter.** "Yes", "Oh ok", "Cool", with no follow-up or question in return.
- **Delays that grow longer**, when you had found a regular rhythm.
- **Always the same person restarting things.** If you are the one opening every exchange, the balance has been lost.
- **Topics going round in circles**: the weather, work, "did you have a good day?".
- **A feeling of interrogation**, where questions follow one another without really picking up on the answers.

A single sign doesn't mean much: everyone has busy days. It is the repetition that should alert you.

## Why it happens

The reasons are often ordinary, and rarely dramatic:

- **You have covered the basic questions.** Job, city, family, hobbies: once those are done, you have to go further, and that takes effort.
- **Writing has its limits.** There is no voice, no eye contact, no shared laughter. Many people tire of an exchange that never moves on to the next step.
- **Life gets in the way**: work, family, travel, network outages.
- **Interest drops** on one side or both, which is also useful information.

## Ways to revive it that work

### Open questions

A closed question calls for a yes or a no. An open question invites a story. Compare:

- "Do you like cooking?" versus "What's the dish you make best, and who taught you?"
- "Do you travel a lot?" versus "Which place has stayed with you the most, and why?"
- "Did you have a good weekend?" versus "What made you smile this weekend?"

The best questions touch on **memories, wishes and small stories**, not just facts.

### Shared memories

Coming back to a detail from your own conversation shows you were listening, and it is a quiet compliment:

- "You told me about your grandmother who made the best attiéké in the neighbourhood (the cassava couscous so loved in Côte d'Ivoire). Did you ever get her recipe?"
- "So, that interview you were nervous about, how did it go?"

This kind of follow-up is warm and personal, and it is almost impossible to answer in a single word.

### The profile as a source of ideas

Go back to the person's profile: their bio, their **interests**, their **values**, their answers to the **profile questions**. There is often a subject you haven't explored yet. Crushduo's **icebreakers** and **first-message coach** draw on exactly these elements: they can also inspire you to revive a conversation that has already started.

### A change of tone

Sometimes all it takes is breaking the routine:

- **A playful question**: "If you could have dinner with anyone, living or not, who would you choose?"
- **A little challenge**: "Describe your ideal week in three words."
- **Sharing something personal**: tell them about something that happened to you, and let them respond.

### Suggesting a call or a date

This is often the most effective way to revive things. A written conversation that is running out of steam may simply need a change of format:

- "I get the feeling we'd understand each other better out loud. Fancy a call one of these evenings?"
- "We've been talking about that maquis for a week. Shall we go and check it out together?" (a maquis is a local open-air eatery)

If you both want to, you can **propose a date in the conversation** and use **Share with someone I trust** to send the place and time to a loved one, so you can go out with peace of mind. Always choose a public place for a first meeting.

## What drives people away

Some follow-ups, however well meant, have the opposite effect:

- **Reproaches**: "You don't reply any more, it's hurtful."
- **Strings of messages** sent without waiting for an answer.
- **Empty nudges**: "?", "Hello?", "Are you there?".
- **Overplaying it**: pretending to be swamped or indifferent to spark interest.

Simplicity and sincerity remain your best allies.

## Knowing when to let go

You have tried once, then a second time from another angle, and the other person still replies half-heartedly? It may be time to let it go. That is not a failure: some connections are meant to last a few days, no more.

A few respectful options:

- **Say it simply**: "I get the feeling the spark isn't really there any more, and that's fine. Thanks for our chats, I wish you all the best."
- **Leave the door ajar**, if you mean it: "If you ever feel like picking things up again, I'd be happy to hear from you."
- **Take a break** with **Pause mode** if you feel a more general weariness.

Letting go of a conversation that no longer feeds you frees up time and energy for the ones with real potential.

## A question of balance

A good conversation is like a game of catch: each person throws back, each person keeps it going. If you have been carrying the exchange on your own for too long, it isn't up to you to make all the effort.

> One sincere follow-up is worth more than ten messages sent to fill the silence.

So next time you feel the conversation slowing down, don't panic. Reread what the other person has shared with you, ask a real question, perhaps suggest a call. And if nothing picks up, say thank you, close kindly, and keep your enthusiasm intact for the next connection: it is part of what makes you lovable.
